What Credit Score Do You Need to Buy a Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 17, 2026 · 8 min read

There's no official SBA minimum credit score anymore — the SBSS prescreen that used to gate small 7(a) loans retired on March 1, 2026. What's left is each lender's own bar, and in practice the market has settled around 680 personal FICO for acquisition loans. Flexible lenders will work at 650 or the mid-600s; conservative banks want 700+. But the score only opens the door. The approval comes from cash flow: whether the business you're buying can cover the loan payment with room to spare.

The bar, lender by lender

NerdWallet's lender survey puts typical 7(a) minimums at 650+, with real spread: Live Oak Bank at 650, First Federal Bank at 700. SBA 504 loans tend to want 680+, and microloans go to 620 or lower — some programs down to 575. For acquisitions specifically, Port 51's underwriting guide calls 680 the informal floor, with 700–720 the comfortable zone — and notes that a 680 guarantees nothing. It gets the file read, not approved.

The SBA minimum that just disappeared

For years, small 7(a) applications ran through a prescreen score called FICO SBSS — a 0–300 blend of the owners' personal credit, the business's credit, and financial and application data. The bar kept rising: 140 to 155 in October 2020, then 155 to 165 in June 2025, when the small-loan ceiling also dropped from $500K to $350K. Then the whole prescreen sunset: SBA Procedural Notice 5000-875701, issued January 16, 2026 and effective March 1, eliminated the mandatory threshold — Versquare's summary has the details. Lenders now underwrite these like similarly sized conventional loans.

Don't celebrate yet. As Nav points out, many lenders will likely keep running SBSS voluntarily, because it's a tool they already trust. The working assumption: you face the stricter of both worlds.

What's actually in your number

Per myFICO, the weights are payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of history 15%, new credit 10%, and credit mix 10%. You can't fix payment history quickly. You can fix utilization — the amounts-owed slice — in one billing cycle, which makes it the fast lever before an application.

Timing matters too. Pull your own reports three to six months before you start shopping deals, not the week you sign an LOI. That window is long enough to dispute errors, pay down balances, and let the score settle — and short enough that the improvement is still fresh when the lender looks. Opening new cards or financing a truck during the search works against you twice: a new inquiry and a shorter average account age, both in the 25% of the score you were trying to protect.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Dana, FICO 694, buys a $350,000 HVAC business — the Q3 2026 median price. Total project: $388,000 ($350K price + $25K working capital + $13K guaranty fee and closing costs). Her 10% injection: $38,800. Loan: $349,200 at 10% over 10 years = $4,615 a month, or $55,376 a year.

The business runs $156,000 of SDE (the Q3 median cash flow). Subtract the $60,000 salary Dana needs to live: $96,000 available for debt. Coverage: $96,000 ÷ $55,376 = 1.73×, comfortably past the 1.25× lenders want. Notice what her 694 did in that paragraph: nothing. It got the file read. The coverage ratio got it approved.

Hard stops your score won't show

Port 51 lists the killers that live outside FICO: delinquent federal debt — including a defaulted federal student loan or a prior SBA loss — a recent bankruptcy, or an active tax lien. Slow-downs include utilization above 30%, a thin file, and something like ten credit inquiries in three months. One more wrinkle: every 20%+ owner signs a personal guarantee, so your partner's 590 is your problem too. And no score compensates for a business that can't cover its own debt — which is the same test sellers run on buyers in spotting serious buyers.

The below-680 playbook

First, utilization arithmetic: carrying $18,000 on $20,000 of limits is 90%; paying down to $5,500 is 27.5%, and the score response can be fast. Second, cure any federal debt problem before applying — it's a hard stop, not a negotiation. Third, bring a bigger injection than the minimum 10%; cash calms underwriters. Fourth, target deals with 1.5×+ coverage instead of squeakers. Fifth, remember the microloan lane exists for smaller deals. Sixth, shop lenders — the 650-versus-700 spread means the identical file gets approved at one bank and declined at another. While you're at it, make sure the business itself survives due diligence, and sanity-check whether buying beats starting for your situation at all.

The seller-side flip

If you're selling and carrying a note, congratulations: you're the lender now, and this whole article is your underwriting manual. Seller notes default at a reported 11–14%, so pull the buyer's credit the legal way — written authorization, or have them share their own report — and run the same coverage math a bank would. The honest summary: aim for 680, know that 650 can work, and expect 700 to smooth everything. Then go find the right business — our tour of where businesses are listed is the starting line, and the SBA loan guide covers the rest of the financing.

Frequently asked questions

Does the SBA have a minimum credit score?

No — the mandatory SBSS prescreen (last set at 165) ended March 1, 2026. Each lender sets its own bar now, and ~680 personal FICO is the practical floor for acquisitions, with conservative banks wanting 700 or better and flexible shops working down to the mid-600s.

Can I get an SBA loan with a 650?

At some lenders, yes. Expect extra scrutiny, bring strong coverage and a clean federal-debt record, and shop multiple banks — minimums genuinely differ. A decline at one lender is a data point, not a verdict, and it doesn't appear on anyone else's desk.

What is a FICO SBSS score?

A 0–300 small-business blend of personal credit, business credit, and financials. The SBA no longer requires it as of March 2026, but many lenders will keep running it voluntarily, so treat the old 165 bar as still quietly alive.
