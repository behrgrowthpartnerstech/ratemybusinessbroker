Should You Buy the Building When You Buy the Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 22, 2026 · 9 min read

Often, yes — if two bars clear. First: the financing math works, and it works more often than people expect, because a 25-year fixed SBA 504 payment frequently lands at or below the rent you'd pay anyway, while quietly building equity. Second: you can spare the extra down payment, because the building adds real cash to the closing table and makes the whole deal bigger and slower. That's it. Not a philosophy question, a math problem — so let's do the math.

First, untangle the two prices

The business and the building are priced by different markets. The business is SDE times a multiple — Q3 2026 medians run a $350,000 price, a 2.30× multiple, and 93.6% of asking — per the method in how brokers value a business. The building is comps and an appraisal. Never accept one blended number, because blending hides the rent trap: when the seller owns the building and charges themselves below-market rent, the business's SDE is inflated. Say the P&L shows $1,500 a month of rent in a $4,750 market. That's $3,250 a month — $39,000 a year — of fake SDE, which at 2.3× pads the business price by roughly $90,000. Re-state SDE at market rent before you price either asset. The same adjustment runs in reverse when a seller has been overcharging themselves rent to shift money into the building entity — then the business is better than its books. Either way, one question unlocks it: what would this space rent for to a stranger?

The 504: how owner-occupied buildings actually get bought

The SBA 504 structure, per NerdWallet's guide: a bank funds 50%, a Certified Development Company funds 40% through a debenture, and you put down 10%. The CDC piece is fixed for 25 years — the November 5, 2026 debenture sale priced at an effective 6.57% — and the program's own numbers say how it's used: median 504 loan $748,500, with 94.1% of borrowers taking 25-year terms. The rules that matter: you must occupy 51% of an existing building (60% for new construction); the 504 can't fund the business price, goodwill, or working capital, so a 7(a) loan rides in parallel for the business side; the down payment escalates to 15% if the business is under two years old or the property is special-purpose, 20% if both; and fees run about 3% of the debenture, mostly financeable, with funding in roughly 60 days per the CDC Loans FAQ.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Amara is buying a machine shop: SDE $170,000 after fair-market rent of $4,750/mo ($57,000/yr). The seller wants $400,000 for the business and $600,000 for the building. Either way, the business runs on a 7(a): project $440,000 ($400K + $25K WC + $15K closing), injection $44,000, loan $396,000 at 10%/10yr = $5,233/mo ($62,798/yr).

Option A — lease: cash available $170,000 − $60,000 salary = $110,000 → coverage on the 7(a) alone: 1.75. Cash at close: $44,000.

Option B — buy the building too: bank half $300,000 at 8%/25yr = $2,315/mo, CDC $240,000 at 6.57%/25yr = $1,631/mo. Building total: $3,946/mo = $47,358/yr — $9,600 less than the rent. Add the rent back to cash flow: $167,000 available against $110,156 of total debt service → coverage 1.52. Cash at close: $104,000 ($44K + $60K down on the building). And about $7,800 of year-one principal comes back as equity.

When buying the building is wrong

Four honest reasons to lease. The down payment drains your reserve — Amara's Option B costs $60,000 more at closing, and a business with no cushion is a worse investment than a building is a good one. The building is the wrong size — if you can't occupy 51%, the 504 is off the table, and buying a building to become a reluctant landlord is a second job. The property is special-purpose, which bumps the down payment to 15% and narrows your future buyers. Or the timeline bites: appraisal, Phase I environmental, and CDC processing add weeks, and a seller in a hurry may walk. One clean fix — negotiate the building as a separate, later closing. Nothing requires both deals to sign the same afternoon, and our what-conveys guide covers keeping the assets straight.

If you don't buy it, nail the lease

A business without site control is a business with an expiration date. Per BizBuySell's landlord guide, buyers (and their lenders) want 3+ years of remaining term, and assignment fees typically run $2,000–$5,000. When the seller keeps the building as your landlord, get it all in writing before closing: a 3–5 year term with options, rent fixed at the same number used in the SDE math — no post-closing surprises — and ideally a right of first refusal if they ever sell the building. The lease-assignment guide runs the whole checklist.

The long game

A 25-year fixed payment never gets a rent increase, and that spread compounds: today's $9,600 advantage grows every time the market reprices. Down the road you can sell the business and keep the building — becoming the landlord who collects from the next owner, which is the quietest retirement plan in small business. Most buyers hold the real estate in a separate entity that leases to the operating company; it's routine, but it has tax and liability wrinkles, so run the structure past your CPA and attorney — this is general information, not advice. And interview your broker on exactly this split: a broker who can't explain the two-price rule on a financed deal is telling you something.

Frequently asked questions

Can an SBA loan cover the business and the building together?

As a package, yes: a 504 for the real estate (50/40/10) and a parallel 7(a) for the business price and working capital. The 504 alone can't fund goodwill or the business.

How much is the down payment on a 504?

10% standard; 15% if the business is under two years old or the building is special-purpose; 20% if both. Fees ≈3% of the debenture, mostly financeable.

Should I lease or buy the premises?

Buy when the 25-year fixed payment is at or below market rent, the reserve survives the down payment, and you'll occupy 51%+. Lease when cash is tight — and then lock 3+ years of term with options.
