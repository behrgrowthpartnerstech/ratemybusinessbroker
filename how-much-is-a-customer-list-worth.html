How Much Is Your Customer List Worth When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Your customer list is worth whatever portion of the sale price the revenue behind it can defend — no more. On a Main Street deal, nobody writes a separate check for the list. It's baked into the price the whole business fetches: SDE times a multiple, and the Q3 2026 market medians are a $350,000 sale price at a 2.30× multiple. Recurring accounts on contracts, with CRM history to prove they pay on time, are the engine of that price. A spreadsheet of one-off names from 2019 is worth roughly what it weighs.

The list is already in the price

When a buyer pays 2.3 times earnings for your business, they're making a bet that your customers keep showing up after your name comes off the door. The multiple is the list valuation, in disguise. That's why retention moves price more than size does. Eton Venture Services treats roughly 85% annual retention as the mark of a genuinely strong customer asset — the kind that earns a multiple at the top of the range instead of the bottom.

Concentration cuts the other way. JMCO's valuation team notes that buyers and lenders start treating a single customer at around 10% of revenue as a risk, and companies where one or two customers top 50% of revenue have sometimes failed to sell at all. A big list dominated by one whale isn't a big list. It's one customer wearing a list as a costume. If that account sits on your ledger, fixing the concentration a year or two before listing is worth more than any appraisal method.

How appraisers put a number on it

When a list does get valued on its own — larger deals, purchase-price allocations, disputes — appraisers reach for three methods. Excess earnings asks what profit the list generates after every other asset gets paid a fair return on its value. Relief from royalty asks what you'd have to pay to license an equivalent list if you didn't own one. Replacement cost asks what it would cost in marketing and sales effort to rebuild the list from zero.

All three reward the same things: customers who buy repeatedly, at good margins, without being romanced back every quarter. A thousand high-margin recurring accounts beat five thousand low-margin one-timers on every method. And age matters — a five-year-old list of bounced emails appraises at approximately nothing. Size flatters. Retention pays.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Irene runs a commercial pool-service company: 400 accounts at $115 a month, which is $46,000 a month or $552,000 a year of revenue. Her SDE is $160,000, and at 2.4× she's asking $384,000.

Here's the math a careful buyer runs. At 90% annual retention, 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 72.9% of accounts remain after three years — $402,408 of today's revenue still arriving in year three. At 70% retention, only 34.3% survive: $189,336. Same list, same 400 names, and a $213,000-a-year gap by year three. The replacement-cost floor runs the other way: if winning a commercial account costs about $450 in sales and marketing, rebuilding the list from scratch is 400 × $450 = $180,000. Retention is the whole argument.

What moves a list up or down

Contracts beat relationships, and relationships beat habits. A signed service agreement with auto-renewal is an asset a lender can see. A customer who "always calls us" is a hope. One caveat on contracts: many carry anti-assignment clauses, which means they don't transfer without the customer's consent — our guide to what happens to contracts in a sale covers that staircase.

Recurring revenue beats project revenue; a buyer pays more for 400 monthly accounts than for 400 completed jobs. Owner-attached relationships are the quiet discount: if the top twenty customers think of you as the company, the buyer sees key-man risk and prices it. And proof beats stories. A CRM export showing tenure, frequency, and payment history defends the price; "everybody loves us" doesn't — the same logic behind the paperwork checklist. Through all of it, the names themselves stay confidential until late in the process, which is exactly what a blind listing is for.

The tax line nobody reads until April

At closing, the purchase price gets allocated on IRS Form 8594, and the customer list gets its own line. For the buyer, it barely matters where the dollars land between "customer-based intangibles" and "goodwill" — Section 197 amortizes both over 15 years. For the seller, amounts allocated to the list are generally capital gain, which is the friendly rate. The allocation still deserves attention, because other lines on that form are taxed worse. This isn't tax advice; it's a reason to call your CPA before you sign, not after.

Selling the list by itself

Some lists trade without the business attached: insurance books of business, accounting and tax client lists, delivery and service routes. Pricing there keys off the revenue that actually transfers — usually with a lookback period or holdback, so the price adjusts if clients walk. Two warnings. First, the buyer is buying transfer, not history, so expect the deal to pay out over a year or two. Second, read your own privacy policy. If you told customers "we never share your data," that promise sells with the list, and a lawyer should look before the names change hands.

Frequently asked questions

How do you value a customer list?

Three appraisal methods: excess earnings, relief from royalty, and replacement cost. On Main Street deals the list usually isn't valued separately at all — it's embedded in the SDE multiple, and provable retention is what pushes that multiple up. Our guide to how brokers value a business shows where the multiple comes from.

Is my customer list included when I sell?

Yes — it transfers with the business in a standard asset sale and gets a line on the Form 8594 allocation. Contracts with anti-assignment clauses are the exception; those need customer consent to move.

Can I sell the list without the business?

In list-driven industries, yes: insurance books, accounting clients, routes. Price follows the revenue that transfers, usually with a retention lookback. Check your privacy policy before the names move.
