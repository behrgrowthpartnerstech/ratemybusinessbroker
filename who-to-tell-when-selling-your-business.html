Who Should You Tell That You're Selling Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 11, 2026 · 8 min read

Spouse, CPA, and attorney first — and the CPA years early, not weeks. Everyone else gets told on a schedule: buyers only after the NDA, the landlord before diligence starts, the bank around signing, employees near closing, customers after. The order exists because telling some people late costs money, and telling others early costs more.

The inner circle, embarrassingly early

Your spouse, for the obvious reasons. Your CPA, because the money is made years out: lenders want 2–3 years of returns that match the financial statements, add-backs need a paper trail built over time (see the full document checklist), and entity-level moves have multi-year clocks — more on that in a moment. Your attorney, to read the lease, the key contracts, and any partnership agreement before a buyer's lawyer finds the clause you forgot existed (the consent mess is covered in do contracts transfer).

Worked example (hypothetical, simplified)

Walt's company is a C corporation; an asset sale produces a $400,000 gain, essentially all goodwill. The C-corp math: 21% corporate tax = $84,000, then the remaining $316,000 distributed and taxed at 15% = $47,400. Total: $131,400 — about a third of the gain.

If Walt had told his CPA five years earlier, an S election would have started the five-year built-in-gains clock — expired by sale day, leaving a single 15% layer: $60,000. The early phone call was worth $71,400. (Simplified: state tax, basis, and the 3.8% NIIT ignored.)

The attorney's early read is cheap and specific: does the lease allow assignment, and on what terms? Does any customer contract carry a change-of-control clause? If there's a partner, what does the operating agreement say about a sale — right of first refusal, drag-along, valuation formula? Each of those is a ten-minute read now or a five-figure surprise under exclusivity later. Early also means the attorney you use is one you chose, not one you grabbed in a week-nine panic because the buyer's counsel sent a sixty-page draft and yours was due back Friday.

Buyers: the name comes after the signature

Buyers learn your business exists through a blind listing, and learn its name only after signing an NDA and passing qualification. A typical process collects 10–30 NDAs, and about half of those people vanish after first contact — which is the screen doing its job, not a problem to fix.

One disclosure that goes the other direction: a business partner. If you have one, the operating agreement probably decides what you're even allowed to sell and when — rights of first refusal, drag-along and tag-along clauses, sometimes a valuation formula that overrides the market. That conversation happens before the broker's, because a partner who learns about your exit from a blind listing becomes an obstacle with legal standing. Partners first, then the professionals, then everyone else on the schedule.

The landlord: early, but not first

This is the order most sellers get wrong. Per BizBuySell's landlord guidance: discuss intentions early — you don't need a buyer in hand to ask how assignments work — and do not wait until due diligence, because a surprised landlord can stall you right up to escrow. Assignment consent runs 60–90 days, fees typically $2,000–$5,000, and buyers want three-plus years left on the lease. While you're at it, ask about getting your personal guarantee released. The full playbook is in what happens to the lease.

The bank: when the paperwork is real

Your lender's role is mechanical — payoff letters and lien releases at closing — so the right moment is around the signed purchase agreement, with your attorney steering the sequence. Not earlier: a listing runs a median 155 days per the Insight Report, and plenty of listings never become sales. There's no prize for giving your banker five months of advance anxiety.

Employees and customers: last, and that's the kind version

It feels disloyal, and it's the opposite. By common industry estimates only 20–30% of listings ever sell (what happens otherwise is covered in when a business doesn't sell) — telling your team about every maybe means years of churn over deals that mostly don't happen. The standard sequence: one key manager under NDA late in diligence if the buyer needs them; everyone else at or near closing; customers the next business day, with the buyer present. How to run a quiet process is in selling without employees finding out, and what the sale means for the team in what happens to your employees.

When the day comes, how you tell the team matters as much as when. The standard script works because it answers the only three questions anyone has: what happens to my job (usually nothing — the buyer bought the business because of the team), what happens to my pay and benefits (say it precisely), and who do I work for now (introduce them, in person, that day). Vendors get calls the same week, keyed to how critical they are. The goal of the whole sequence isn't secrecy for its own sake — it's that every person hears the news from you, with the answer to their question attached, instead of from the parking lot.

One more name for the early list: the broker — they're the one building the blind profile and running the screen (see the full job description). Just vet them like you'd vet a buyer. Reviews help.

Frequently asked questions

Should I tell my accountant I'm selling?

Yes, years early. Matching returns, documented add-backs, and five-year entity clocks all pay out only if the CPA knew in time. Cheapest money in the whole deal.

When should I tell my landlord?

Early — before diligence. No buyer needed to ask about the assignment process. Expect 60–90 days for consent, a $2,000–$5,000 fee, and buyer demands for 3+ years of lease term.

Should I tell my bank?

At the signed purchase agreement, with your attorney steering. Their job is payoff letters and lien releases at closing — not five months of watching your listing.

General information, not tax or legal advice — entity and disclosure decisions belong with your CPA and attorney, and the right order for your sale depends on your lease, your contracts, and your people. The schedule above is the default, not a statute; the principle underneath it travels everywhere: tell each party exactly when their help becomes more valuable than their worry.
