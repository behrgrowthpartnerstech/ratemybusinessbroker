How Do Business Brokers Value a Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 17, 2026 · 8 min read

A broker values a Main Street business in three moves. First, recast the financials: take the tax returns and P&L and rebuild them into seller's discretionary earnings — net profit plus owner salary plus documented add-backs. Second, apply a market multiple pulled from databases of actual closed sales; for businesses under $1M the average currently sits at 2.30×, per the BizBuySell Insight Report. Third — the step that separates professionals from optimists — stress-test the price against lender math, because a price a financed buyer can't cover isn't a price, it's a wish. Everything else in a valuation conversation is commentary on those three moves.

Step one: the recast is most of the work

Small-business books are written to minimize taxes, so the raw net profit understates what an owner actually takes home. The recast adds back the owner's salary and payroll taxes, perks run through the business, interest, depreciation, and true one-time costs — each one needing paper behind it, because every add-back gets re-litigated by the buyer's accountant and the buyer's lender. A $10,000 add-back that dies in diligence takes $23,000 of price with it at a 2.3× multiple. The recast is also where honest brokers earn trust and where careless ones plant the seeds of a broken deal six months later.

Step two: the multiple comes from comps, not vibes

Professionals pull comparable-sale data from DealStats, BizComps, PeerComps, and BizBuySell's own closed-transaction records, then adjust for what the databases can't see: revenue trend, customer concentration, owner dependence, lease quality. Industry matters enormously — restaurant multiples and HVAC multiples live in different neighborhoods (see why industry experience matters). Size matters more: multiples climb with earnings because bigger businesses depend less on one owner. And the market's gravity is strong: under-$1M businesses sold at 93.6% of asking in Q3 2026, while over-$1M deals averaged 100.8% — competition can beat ask at the top, while overpricing at the bottom mostly produces silence.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Teresa's bakery recasts to $212,000 SDE. Comps for bakeries her size cluster around 2.5×, suggesting a value of $212,000 × 2.5 = $530,000. Her broker lists at $549,000 — a touch above, leaving room to negotiate.

Reality behaves like the statistics say: after one price conversation with a qualified buyer, the deal signs at roughly the market's 93.6% of ask — $549,000 × 0.936 ≈ $514,000, right on top of the comp-derived value. The lender check confirms it: a 90% loan of $462,600 at 10% over 10 years costs about $73,340 a year, and $212,000 of SDE minus a $75,000 manager-equivalent salary leaves $137,000 — coverage of 1.87×. Financeable, defensible, done. That's what a valuation that holds looks like: three independent methods landing within a few percent of each other.

The BOV deserves one extra sentence of cynicism: because it's free and doubles as a listing pitch, some brokers inflate it to win your signature, knowing the market will deliver the bad news later. A BOV meaningfully above what comps and lender math support isn't flattery — it's bait, and it costs you months. The quick-math version takes five minutes and makes you harder to flatter.

Step three: the lender check, and why it rules them all

Most Main Street buyers finance the purchase, so every price implicitly makes a promise: the business's cash flow will cover the buyer's loan payments with room to spare — banks want roughly 1.25× coverage, and the SBA floor is 1.15×. A broker who prices without running this arithmetic is pricing for a cash buyer who probably doesn't exist. The SBA adds its own referee: an independent valuation is required on every financed change of ownership, so an inflated ask gets repriced by a stranger mid-deal — after you've mentally spent the bigger number. Price to survive the referee and the timing question becomes the only one left.

When you interview brokers, make the valuation method the centerpiece: which comp databases, which add-backs survive, what the lender math says, and how the ask relates to the BOV. Vague answers to those four questions predict a stale listing better than any credential predicts a sale.

What moves a multiple up or down

Two businesses with identical SDE can deserve different multiples, and the adjustments follow a pattern any buyer will recognize. Upward pressure: revenue growing over multiple years, recurring or contracted income, a diversified customer base, a manager or team that runs the place without you, clean documented books, and a long lease at market rent. Downward pressure: a trend pointing the wrong way, one customer over ten or fifteen percent of revenue, the owner as chief rainmaker and only keyholder, add-backs that live on a napkin, and a month-to-month lease. None of this is mysterious — each factor is a proxy for how likely the earnings are to survive the ownership change, which is the only thing a buyer is actually purchasing. The practical upshot for sellers: most multiple-moving problems are fixable in the year before listing, which is precisely why the 12-month preparation plan exists and why brokers who raise these issues early are worth more than brokers who promise a bigger number.

Frequently asked questions

How do brokers determine what a business is worth?

Recast to SDE, apply a comp-derived multiple (2.30× average under $1M), and stress-test against lender coverage math. Three methods that should land within a few percent of each other.

What does a valuation cost?

A broker's opinion of value is usually free — it's their pitch. Certified appraisals run ~$5K into the $10–20K+ range and are what the IRS, courts, and SBA rely on.

Do businesses sell for asking price?

Under $1M, the average is 93.6% of ask; over $1M, 100.8% — auctions can beat ask. Badly overpriced listings don't sell at a discount; they don't sell.
