Do I Need an NDA to Sell My Business? What It Protects — and What It Doesn't

By The Broker Index Team · November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — you need an NDA, and every serious buyer will sign one before they ever learn your business's name. It's the first document signed in a sale, ahead of the financials, ahead of the offer, ahead of everything. A typical sell-side process collects 10 to 30 signed NDAs from curious buyers before one of them closes. The real questions aren't whether to use one. They're what a good one says, what it can actually do for you, and what it can't.

Where the NDA sits in the process

A confidential sale runs as a staircase. The buyer sees a blind listing first: business type, region, banded numbers, no name. If they want more, they sign the NDA and answer qualification questions about cash and experience. Only then do they get the name and the CIM — the full information package. Customer names and contract-level detail wait even longer, until after a signed letter of intent.

Roughly half of inquirers vanish after first contact, and that's fine. The NDA isn't just protection; it's a screen. People who won't sign a two-page agreement weren't going to wire six figures. The drop-out rate is a feature, not a leak in your funnel — it's the same logic as checking proof of funds before handing over your tax returns.

What a good one actually says

Most broker templates cover the same bones, and the bones matter: a broad definition of confidential information, a permitted-use clause (evaluation of this purchase, nothing else), a term — typically 2–3 years, though Morgan & Westfield puts the common range at 1–5 — plus return-or-destroy obligations, remedies, and governing law.

Three details separate a good NDA from a photocopied one. First, it should cover the fact of the sale itself, both ways, with an expiration date — the buyer can't tell anyone your business is on the market, and per BizBuySell's guidance that obligation should be explicit, not implied. Second, a non-solicitation clause: 12–24 months of hands off your employees, plus a no-hire provision. Third, the right signatures — the buying entity and the individual behind it, so a dissolved LLC doesn't take your protection down with it. One tell worth knowing: private equity firms sign NDAs all day long. VCs usually won't. A buyer's attitude toward your two-page NDA predicts their attitude toward your twenty-page purchase agreement.

The clause that pays for the whole document

The non-solicit of customers and employees is where the money lives, because the worst-case leak isn't gossip — it's a "buyer" who was really shopping for your accounts.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Omar runs a specialty-foods distributor: revenue $1,200,000, SDE $200,000, asking $200,000 × 2.4 = $480,000. Two anchor accounts make up 25% of revenue — $300,000 — and at a 32% contribution margin they carry $96,000 of his SDE.

A rival poses as a buyer, learns the account names, and poaches both. Omar's SDE drops to $104,000, and at the same 2.4× his business is now worth $249,600. The leak cost $230,400 — about 48% of the price. The NDA review that guards against it costs $380. That's roughly a 600-to-1 ratio, which is the best deal anywhere in this industry.

What it won't do

Be honest with yourself about enforcement. If a buyer breaches, an injunction typically costs $50,000–$200,000 and takes 30–90 days. Full litigation runs $500,000 to $2 million-plus over one to three years. Liquidated-damages clauses show up on larger deals precisely because proving actual damages is miserable. By the time you win, the customers are gone.

So the NDA's real job is deterrence and discipline, not armor. The discipline half is yours: release information in phases, number your CIM copies so a leaked document points back at its recipient, and keep customer names out of everything until the LOI is signed. A signature plus a paper trail makes a would-be leaker think twice. That's most of the value, and it costs you nothing but process.

Competitors get the strict version

Sometimes the best buyer really is the rival across town — they understand the business and can pay for synergies. They also have the most to gain from a fishing trip. For competitor buyers, brokers use a tighter, buyer-specific NDA: it covers oral and derived information (notes, models, anything built from your data), names the specific individuals allowed to see the file with the buyer entity liable for all of them, adds a no-hire clause, and holds the customer list in escrow until closing. If a competitor balks at the strict version, you've learned what the visit was really about — cheaply.

What it costs, and who drafts it

This is one of the cheapest pieces of insurance in the whole deal. Per ContractsCounsel's marketplace data, a flat-fee NDA review averages $380, drafting from scratch about $510, with hourly rates of $200–$350. In practice your broker supplies the template and your attorney gives it one pass — MidStreet's guidance is exactly that division of labor. If you're selling without a broker, pay the $510. If you've signed a listing agreement, the NDA process is part of what the fee buys — and how carefully a broker runs it is a fair test of the rest of their process. It's also one more reason to check how a broker handled confidentiality for past sellers before you build your deal team.

Frequently asked questions

Is an NDA enforceable when selling a business?

Yes — courts enforce well-drafted ones. But enforcement is slow and expensive (injunctions $50–200K over 30–90 days; litigation $500K–$2M+ over 12–36 months), so the practical value is deterrence plus process: phased release, numbered CIMs, traceable signatures.

When does the buyer sign the NDA?

Before learning the business's name. Blind listing first, then NDA plus qualification, then the name and CIM. Customer-level detail waits until after a signed LOI. Expect 10–30 signed NDAs across a full process.

What if a buyer refuses to sign?

That's your answer. Serious buyers and PE firms sign routinely. Someone who won't commit to confidentiality before seeing your numbers is showing you how they'd treat them after. You can also stop worrying about backing out of a conversation that never should have started.
