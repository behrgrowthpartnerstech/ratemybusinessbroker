What Happens to Your Employees When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 29, 2026 · 9 min read

In most small-business sales, your employees keep their jobs — and the paperwork says otherwise for exactly one day. Because most Main Street deals are asset sales, the buyer purchases the business's assets rather than your legal entity, so employees are technically terminated by your company at closing and rehired by the buyer's company the same afternoon, usually at the same desk and wage. The buyer wants them: the staff is the business they just paid for. The real action is in the details — final paychecks, PTO, health coverage, who gets told when, and whether the two or three people who actually run the place stay through the handover.

The mechanics: terminated at 10 a.m., rehired by lunch

In an asset sale, you pay final wages and accrued PTO through closing — many states require the payout by law — and your health plan ends with your entity, with COBRA continuation available for 18 months or more. The buyer onboards everyone fresh: new W-4s, new handbook, benefits per their plan. In a stock sale, the entity survives, so employment simply continues. Either way, the purchase agreement should name who pays what — unpaid PTO, payroll taxes through closing, pending bonuses — because every dollar left vague becomes a closing-week argument. (It's one of the standard line items on closing day.)

One legal tripwire for larger sellers: the federal WARN Act requires 60 days' notice before mass layoffs at companies with 100+ full-time employees when 50+ workers lose jobs at a site. Most Main Street sellers are nowhere near the threshold, but if a buyer plans consolidation after acquiring a larger operation, WARN belongs on the lawyers' checklist — and several states run stricter "mini-WARN" versions.

The turnover problem buyers price in

People leave after acquisitions at multiples of normal rates. An MIT Sloan analysis of Census data found roughly 33% of acquired employees gone within a year, versus 12% ordinary attrition — a tech-startup-heavy sample, so Main Street numbers likely run gentler, but the direction is universal: change of control shakes people loose. Buyers know this, which is why "will the staff stay?" is a diligence question with a price attached. High owner-dependence plus flight-risk staff equals a lower multiple or an earnout tied to retention.

Source: MIT Sloan Management Review.

Stay bonuses: small money defending big money

The fix is unromantic and effective: pay the people who matter to stay. Small-business stay bonuses typically run 10–30% of base salary, with 15–25% over 6–12 months the common shape — half at closing, half at the end of the retention period. Upmarket, the practice is near-universal: WTW's 2024 M&A retention survey found 86% of acquirers pay cash retention to senior leaders, at medians of 75–100% of salary for C-suite and about 30% for other salaried staff. Sellers and buyers usually split the cost, since the seller needs the deal to close and the buyer needs the business to still work afterward.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Alma sells her bakery-café for $350,000 with 11 employees. Two matter most: the kitchen manager ($52,000 base) and the front-of-house lead ($44,000). Stay bonuses at 15% through 90 days past closing: $7,800 + $6,600 = $14,400, split 50/50 with the buyer — Alma's share $7,200.

Add her legally required payout of accrued PTO — 240 hours across the team at an average $18/hour = $4,320. Alma's total people-cost of selling: $7,200 + $4,320 = $11,520, about 3.3% of the price — to protect the two humans whose departure would have cratered the buyer's confidence, the transition, and possibly the deal itself.

When and how to tell the team

The standard advice holds: employees learn at or very near closing, customers the next business day, with the buyer present and the message rehearsed — Viking Mergers' timeline is the classic reference. Telling the whole team early feels honorable and routinely backfires: deals fall through (only a fraction of listings ever close), and a staff that spent four months job-hunting over a sale that died is pure damage. The exception is the key manager a buyer will insist on meeting — read them in late in diligence, under NDA, ideally with their stay bonus on the same page of paper. The full playbook for keeping the sale quiet until then is in how to sell without employees finding out.

When you do announce, employees ask three questions in this order: Do I still have a job? Does my pay change? Who do I report to? Have the buyer answer all three in the first five minutes, in writing if possible. Everything else — culture, plans, vision — can wait a week. A broker who has managed this moment dozens of times is worth their fee that morning alone; it's a fair thing to ask about when interviewing brokers.

The seller's quiet obligations

Two more items belong on your side of the ledger. Unwritten promises: the verbal "you'll always have a job here," the handshake bonus, the flexible-schedule deal nobody documented. Buyers don't inherit promises they never saw, so either paper them before the sale or tell the affected employee the truth before someone else does. And references: you're the last person who can vouch for people the buyer may eventually let go. Writing two or three strong reference letters before closing costs an evening and is, for some employees, worth more than the stay bonus. It's also the last managerial act of your ownership, which is reason enough to do it well.

Frequently asked questions

Do employees keep their jobs when a business is sold?

Usually yes — buyers need the staff. In an asset sale they're formally terminated and rehired by the buyer the same day; in a stock sale employment continues untouched. Expect elevated turnover in year one regardless.

Who pays out employee PTO and benefits when a business sells?

The seller typically pays final wages and accrued PTO through closing; the seller's health plan ends with COBRA available; the buyer onboards onto their own plans. The purchase agreement should name every handoff.

What is a stay bonus when selling a business?

A retention payment — typically 15–25% of base over 6–12 months in small businesses, often split between seller and buyer — paid to key employees who stay through closing and transition.
