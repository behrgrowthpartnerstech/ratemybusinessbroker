Do I Have to Sign a Non-Compete When I Sell My Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — expect to sign one, and expect it to stick. Nearly every business sale includes a seller non-compete, and unlike the employee versions you've read headlines about, sale-of-business non-competes are enforceable in all 50 states. Even California, famously hostile to employment non-competes, carves out an explicit exception when someone sells a business and its goodwill. The logic is hard to argue with: the buyer just paid you a multiple of earnings for your customer relationships. A seller who opens up across the street Monday morning is selling the same goodwill twice. So the real questions aren't whether — they're how long, how wide, and, quietly the biggest one, how the dollars get taxed.

Why the headlines don't apply to you

In 2024 the FTC issued a rule banning most employment non-competes; in September 2025 a federal court vacated it, and enforcement now runs case-by-case under Section 5 — all of it about employees, per the American Staffing Association's tracking. Sale-of-business covenants were expressly excluded from the rule even before it died. California makes the same split in statute: Business and Professions Code §16601 permits a seller of a business and its goodwill to agree not to compete in the area where the business operated. Courts apply a reasonableness test to sale covenants and pass them routinely, because the seller got paid for exactly what's being protected.

What's standard, what's negotiable

Per Morgan & Westfield's covenant guidance, typical terms run three to five years, scoped to the industry the business is in and the geography where it actually competes. Everything inside those bounds is negotiable: carve-outs for your other existing business, for passive investments (owning index funds that hold a competitor shouldn't be a breach), for serving on boards, for a defined list of activities you plan to continue. SBA lenders provide a useful outer marker — guidance flagged by Starfield & Smith treats terms beyond five years as often excessive. If a buyer wants ten years and three states you've never operated in, that's not protection, that's a land grab, and a decent lawyer trims it in one redline.

Also in the family: non-solicits (don't poach the employees or customers) and confidentiality covenants. Buyers sometimes accept a tight non-solicit in exchange for a narrower non-compete. If you're staying on through a transition period, the consulting agreement and the covenant need to agree with each other about what you're allowed to do.

The tax line nobody reads until April

When the purchase price gets allocated across asset classes on Form 8594, any dollars tagged to the non-compete are ordinary income to you. Dollars tagged to goodwill are long-term capital gain — 15% for most sellers. The buyer usually doesn't care: under Section 197 they amortize a covenant over 15 years no matter how long it runs (the Recovery Group case settled that), exactly as they amortize goodwill. So the allocation is close to free for them and expensive for you — which means it's negotiable, and you should negotiate it toward a small, defensible number.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Hannah sells her courier company. The buyer's first-draft allocation puts $50,000 on the non-compete. At her 32% ordinary rate, that's $16,000 of tax. Reallocated to goodwill at the 15% capital-gains rate, the same $50,000 costs $7,500.

The swing: $16,000 − $7,500 = $8,500, on a single line, for identical economics. Hannah's CPA pushed the covenant allocation down to a nominal figure, documented why, and both sides filed matching 8594s. Fifteen minutes of attention, $8,500 kept. One caveat: a $0 covenant allocation on a covenant the buyer genuinely values can draw scrutiny — small and defensible beats zero and fictional.

What breaching one actually costs

Sellers occasionally sign assuming the covenant is wallpaper. It isn't. A buyer who can show you're competing gets injunctive relief — a court order shutting your new venture — plus damages, plus, in many agreements, their attorney's fees and the right to stop paying any seller note still outstanding. That last one is the quiet enforcer: if the buyer owes you $150,000 over five years, your own paper is the collateral for your good behavior. Consulting payments tied to a transition get the same leverage. Breach rarely ends in a dramatic courtroom scene; it ends with your payments stopping and your lawyer explaining the offset clause you didn't read.

The honest framing: a non-compete costs you options you probably weren't going to exercise, in exchange for a price you definitely want. Negotiate the scope like it matters — the term, the map, the carve-outs, and above all the allocation. Then honor it and go enjoy the proceeds. Most sellers discover that three years out of the industry they've worked for twenty is less a cage than a vacation with paperwork. And before any of this gets signed, it rides along with the rest of the deal documents in the closing stack — reviewed by your own lawyer, not just the buyer's.

Before you sign: a five-line review

Read the covenant against five questions. Does the restricted business match what you actually sold, or does it sweep in adjacent work you might plausibly do? Does the geography match where the business competes, or the buyer's ambitions? Does the clock start at closing, or restart after any consulting period ends — a quiet way to stretch three years into five? Are your passive investments and existing side ventures carved out by name? And does the breach section let the buyer offset against your note or earnout, which makes them judge and collector in their own case? Five minutes per question with your attorney, before signing, buys you five years of not wondering.

Frequently asked questions

Are non-competes enforceable when you sell a business?

Yes, in all 50 states — including California, via B&P §16601. The FTC's vacated 2024 rule concerned employment covenants and never touched sale-of-business ones. Negotiate scope, not existence.

How long does a seller non-compete last?

Three to five years, scoped to your industry and real geography. SBA guidance treats longer than five years as often excessive — useful leverage when a buyer overreaches.

How is a non-compete payment taxed?

Ordinary income to you; goodwill is capital gain at 15% for most sellers. The buyer amortizes both over 15 years either way, so push the covenant allocation to a small, defensible number.
