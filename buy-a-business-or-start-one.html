Should You Buy a Business or Start One? The Honest Math

By The Broker Index Team · October 25, 2026 · 9 min read

Buy the business if you want income and can raise the down payment; start one if you want something that doesn't exist yet and can afford years without a paycheck. That's the honest one-sentence answer. Buying means paying roughly 2.30× cash flow — the median sold business runs $350,000 — for revenue, staff, and customers that already exist, with a bank financing most of it. Starting means a cheaper entry and total freedom, paid for with the survival curve: about one in two new establishments is gone within five years. Neither is wrong. They're different products: one is an income stream with a mortgage, the other is a lottery ticket you get to help draw.

The survival curve is the whole argument

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks every new establishment. The numbers are remarkably stable across decades: 77.9% survive year one, 51.4% survive five years, 34.7% survive ten. When you buy an established business, you're buying a company that already climbed that curve — the failure risk the founder absorbed is baked into the multiple you pay. When you start one, you're volunteering for the curve yourself, in exchange for not paying the multiple.

What buying actually pays you: the loan math

The acquisition case lives or dies on one calculation: does the existing cash flow cover the loan, your salary, and a margin of safety? Lenders want coverage of about 1.25×; the SBA floor is 1.15. Here's what that looks like on a real-sized deal.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Luis buys a painting contractor at the market medians: $350,000 price, $156,000 SDE. He puts 10% down — $35,000 — and borrows $315,000 at 10.5% over 10 years: about $4,250 a month, $51,005 a year.

He pays himself a salary of $75,000, leaving $156,000 − $75,000 = $81,000 available for debt. Coverage: 81,000 ÷ 51,005 = 1.59 — comfortably bankable. His first-year economics: $75,000 salary + roughly $30,000 left after the loan ≈ $105,000 of total owner benefit, starting the month he takes the keys.

The startup version of Luis spends his $35,000 on a van and equipment, and year one pays him whatever a brand-new customer list pays — often nothing. By year three he may match the acquired company. Or he may be in the 48.6% that didn't make it to year five. The acquisition's premium is the price of skipping that coin flip.

The honest case for starting anyway

Buying isn't automatically smarter. Start instead when any of these is true: the business you want doesn't exist in your market; the industry's listings are junk (tired equipment, fake add-backs — run them through the SDE test and watch them dissolve); your capital genuinely can't stretch to a down payment plus working capital; or the whole point is building a thing your way. Startups also skip acquisition-specific risks: inheriting a diligence surprise, customer lists loyal to the departing owner, and the awkward year of running someone else's systems.

And remember the seller's incentives. Businesses get listed because the owner knows something — sometimes it's "I'm 67," which is fine, and sometimes it's "the anchor client is wobbling," which is not. That's what diligence is for, and it's why serious buyers check the broker's reputation as carefully as the books.

The hybrid nobody mentions: buy small, build on it

The binary framing hides a third path that experienced operators use constantly: buy a modest, boring business as a platform — the customer list, the licenses, the phone number that already rings — and then build your actual vision on top of it. A $200,000 acquisition of a tired two-van cleaning company gets you revenue from week one, a payroll system that works, and a Google profile with nine years of reviews. The founder energy you'd have spent surviving year one goes into growth instead, financed by cash flow that already exists. It's slower to start than a pure startup and cheaper than buying the finished version of your dream, and it converts the survival curve from a threat into a purchase. The catch is discipline: you're buying the base, not falling in love with it, so the diligence bar stays exactly as high as for a bigger deal. Tired businesses hide tired problems, and a platform with a cracked foundation is just a startup with extra steps — the expensive kind, with a loan payment attached from month one.

If you lean buy: three guardrails

First, underwrite like the bank does — coverage at 1.25× or better on documented, not promised, earnings. Second, know where the inventory lives; our tour of every marketplace for businesses saves you weeks. Third, remember the listing broker works for the seller: price opinions, teaser numbers, and "other interested buyers" all come from the sales side of the table. Check the broker's track record and reviews before trusting the process — that's literally why this site exists.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to buy a business or start one?

Starting is cheaper at the door; buying is usually cheaper per dollar of income, because 2.3× cash flow buys earnings that already exist and a bank finances 90% of it.

What percentage of startups fail vs. acquired businesses?

Per BLS: 77.9% of new establishments survive year one, 51.4% survive five years, 34.7% survive ten. An acquisition has already climbed the curve — that's part of what the multiple pays for.

Can I get a loan to buy a business more easily than to start one?

Usually yes. Acquisition lenders underwrite existing cash flow; startup lenders underwrite a forecast. SBA 7(a) routinely finances 90% of a purchase at 1.25× coverage or better.
