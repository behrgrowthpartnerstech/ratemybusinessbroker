What Does CBI Mean After a Business Broker's Name?

By The Broker Index Team · October 14, 2026 · 8 min read

CBI stands for Certified Business Intermediary, the professional credential of the International Business Brokers Association. Earning it requires 52 hours of required coursework in valuation, deal structure, and sale process, a passing exam score, documented brokerage experience, and IBBA membership in good standing; keeping it requires 48 continuing-education credits each recertification cycle. It is not a license, not a guarantee, and not rare enough to be mystical — the IBBA counts more than 3,100 members worldwide, a subset of whom hold the CBI. What it is: evidence a broker invested real hours in learning the mechanics that, when botched, cost sellers real money.

What's actually in the 52 hours

The required sequence is concrete rather than ceremonial: an intro course (4 hours), a core brokerage course or its M&A equivalent (8 hours), pricing small businesses (8 hours), a 16-hour valuation deep-dive, plus deal structure and documentation courses (8 hours each). Add the exam, an experience requirement, and an ethics commitment. Recertification runs on a 48-credit cycle with at least half from educational sources — closed transactions earn credits too, capped so nobody coasts on volume alone. There's a Fast Track for brokers with existing credentials and a half-day refresher option. Details and the member directory live at ibba.org.

Notice what the curriculum is mostly about: pricing. That's not an accident. Valuation is where an untrained broker does the most damage, in either direction.

The mistake the training exists to prevent

Worked example (hypothetical)

Owen wants to sell his sign shop. A broker with no valuation training takes the P&L at face value: $145,000 claimed SDE. But Owen barely works in the business — a full-time manager would cost $65,000 — so the earnings a buyer actually gets are closer to $80,000. The untrained broker lists at $630,000.

A buyer borrows 90%: $567,000 at 10% over 10 years = $89,915 a year of debt service. Against $80,000 of real cash flow, coverage is 0.89×. No lender touches it. The listing sits for a year, goes stale, and eventually sells at a discount that embarrasses everyone.

The trained version: price off the $80,000. At $419,000, a 90% loan of $377,100 costs $59,801 a year; coverage is 80,000 ÷ 59,801 = 1.34× — financeable on day one. Lower sticker, faster sale, and the seller banks money instead of listing fatigue. That's the whole argument for valuation training, in one before-and-after.

A credential is not a license — and most states have neither

Easy to conflate, important not to. The CBI is voluntary and comes from a trade association. Licensing is state law, and the map is a patchwork: roughly 17 states require a real estate license to broker business sales, a couple have registration regimes, and most require nothing at all, per the state licensing guide. So in much of the country, anyone with a phone and optimism can call themselves a business broker tomorrow. That's the context in which a CBI means something: in an unregulated trade, voluntary rigor is one of the few costly signals available. (The licensing landscape and what it means for who you hire gets the full treatment in our real-estate-agent-vs-broker piece.)

How to actually use the credential when hiring

Verify it first — the IBBA directory takes thirty seconds, and a claimed credential that doesn't verify tells you everything. Then treat the CBI as a floor, not a verdict. Ask the eight questions you'd ask any broker: deals closed in the last three years, in your size range, in your industry; how they build a price (the answer must include SDE and defensible add-backs, not "we'll test the market"); and how they'll market confidentially. A CBI who closed two deals ever is a worse bet than a non-CBI who closes twelve a year with happy clients. Credentials answer "did they study?" References and reviews answer "does it work?" You want both answered.

Related alphabet soup you'll meet: M&AMI (the IBBA/M&A Source designation for mid-market intermediaries), CM&AP, and various state association certificates. Same logic applies to all of them — verify, then weigh against actual results. What the CBI doesn't tell you

Three blind spots, so you weight the letters correctly. First, volume: the credential has an experience requirement, but it can't tell you whether the broker closed three deals last year or thirteen, or whether any were in your industry and size range. Transaction history is a separate question, and you ask it separately. Second, incentives: a CBI works on the same success-fee economics as everyone else, which means the same temptation to flatter a valuation to win a listing or push a marginal deal across the line to collect. Training doesn't repeal incentives; it just makes the pricing errors less likely to be accidental. Third, fit: a credentialed generalist in another state may still be the wrong hire against an uncredentialed specialist who has sold nine businesses exactly like yours and knows every likely buyer by first name.

It also can't tell you how the broker behaves when a deal wobbles — whether they return calls in the bad weeks, whether they pressure you toward a marginal buyer at month nine, whether their weekly updates survive past the honeymoon. That temperament data lives only with former clients, which is why reference calls and independent reviews stay on the checklist no matter how decorated the business card gets.

The sensible weighting: treat the CBI as meaningful positive evidence — somebody chose to spend 52 classroom hours and an exam on a trade most people enter with a business card and a hunch. Then make them prove the rest the way you would with anyone: deals, references, reviews, and a valuation walkthrough that shows the training stuck. Letters open the conversation. They don't finish it.

Frequently asked questions

What does CBI stand for?

Certified Business Intermediary — the IBBA's credential. 52 hours of required coursework, an exam, documented experience, and 48 CE credits per cycle to keep it.

Is a CBI the same as a license?

No. It's a voluntary trade-association credential. Licensing is state law: ~17 states require a real estate license for business brokerage; most states require nothing.

Does hiring a CBI guarantee a better sale?

No credential guarantees outcomes. It signals valuation and process training — the failure-prone areas. Check transaction history, references, and independent reviews regardless.
