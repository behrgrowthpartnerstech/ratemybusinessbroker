Asset Sale vs. Stock Sale: Which Is Better When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 4, 2026 · 9 min read

An asset sale means the buyer purchases the contents of your company — equipment, inventory, customer list, goodwill, the name — while the legal entity stays behind with you. A stock sale means they buy the entity itself, shares and all, history included. For deals under $50 million, asset sales are overwhelmingly the norm, and the choice is not paperwork trivia: the structure routinely swings a seller's after-tax proceeds by 10–15% of the purchase price. On a $2 million sale, that's a six-figure difference decided before anyone argues about price.

Why buyers push for asset sales

Taxes and liability — in that order. In an asset sale the buyer gets a stepped-up basis: the price they pay becomes the new tax basis of the assets, which they depreciate, and the goodwill portion amortizes over 15 years under Section 197. Those are real deductions against future profits, worth serious money. In a stock sale the buyer inherits your old, mostly depreciated basis and gets none of that.

Liability is the second push. Buy the assets and, broadly, the skeletons — the old lawsuit, the tax year that was "handled creatively," the warranty claims — stay with your entity. Buy the stock and the skeletons come with the filing cabinet. Buyers remember this; so do their lawyers.

SBA lenders, who finance most Main Street buyers, are comfortable with asset deals as the standard structure. The purchase price gets allocated across asset classes on IRS Form 8594, which both sides must file consistently — and that allocation is its own quiet negotiation, because every dollar steered to equipment (good for the buyer) instead of goodwill (good for you) changes both tax bills.

Why sellers prefer stock sales

In a stock sale, your entire gain is generally taxed once, at long-term capital gains rates — 0, 15 or 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax where it applies. Clean. In an asset sale, the price is carved up: gain on equipment you've depreciated comes back as ordinary-income-rate "recapture," inventory is ordinary, and only goodwill and similar intangibles get capital-gains treatment.

If you're a C corporation, it's worse: the corporation pays tax on the asset gains at the 21% corporate rate, then you pay capital gains tax again when the cash comes out — the famous double tax. That's how effective rates on asset sales reach the 28–35% range against 20–25% for stock sales. C-corp owners holding qualified small business stock have a potential golden ticket — the Section 1202 exclusion can shelter up to $10 million of gain in a qualifying stock sale — which is exactly the kind of thing to know years before you sell, not weeks.

Worked example (hypothetical — illustrative rates; your CPA runs the real numbers)

Maria sells her S-corp manufacturing business for $2,000,000 with negligible basis. Stock sale: the gain is long-term capital gains — 20% federal plus 3.8% NIIT = 23.8%, about $476,000 of tax, netting $1,524,000.

Asset sale: the allocation puts $500,000 on equipment she's fully depreciated (recaptured at her 32% ordinary rate = $160,000) and $1,500,000 on goodwill (23.8% = $357,000). Total tax ≈ $517,000, netting $1,483,000 — $41,000 behind the stock sale even without C-corp double tax. Had she been a C corporation, the asset-sale tax bill would plausibly have passed $600,000. Same business, same handshake price — the structure moved tens of thousands before price negotiations even started.

How the fight usually settles

On Main Street, the buyer mostly wins the structure (asset sale) and the seller gets paid for it — literally. If an asset sale costs you an extra $40,000 in tax, that's a number, and numbers can be negotiated into the price. The standard moves: adjust the headline price to share the tax difference, negotiate the Form 8594 allocation (more goodwill, less equipment recapture), and in some stubborn cases use hybrid structures — like a stock sale with a tax election treated as an asset sale — that give each side part of what it wants. That last category is strictly CPA-and-attorney territory.

Two practical rules. First, get the structure stated in the letter of intent — "asset sale" or "stock sale," in writing — because discovering the buyer's assumption in week ten of exclusivity is how sellers eat six-figure surprises. Second, model your after-tax number under both structures before listing, with your CPA; a broker worth their fee will insist you do. We're a review site, not your tax advisor — this article is a map, and the map is not the terrain. The terrain is your CPA's job.

One more thing the structure touches: what actually conveys. Asset deals itemize — which contracts assign, whether the phone number, licenses, and working capital transfer. Stock deals carry everything automatically, including the things nobody remembered to mention. Both facts cut both ways; the itemized list is work, and the automatic transfer is risk.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an asset sale and a stock sale?

Asset sale: the buyer purchases the business's contents and your entity stays behind. Stock sale: the buyer purchases the entity itself, history and liabilities included. Under $50M, asset sales dominate.

Why do buyers prefer asset sales?

A stepped-up basis — depreciation plus 15-year goodwill amortization under §197 — and liability protection, since old obligations generally stay with the seller's entity.

Which is better for the seller?

Usually a stock sale: one layer of capital-gains tax and liabilities that transfer. The typical gap is 10–15% of the price, which is why structure belongs in the LOI and in your price negotiation.
