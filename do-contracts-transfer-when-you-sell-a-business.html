Do Customer Contracts Transfer When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 4, 2026 · 8 min read

Mostly, no. In an asset sale — the structure most Main Street deals use — contracts don't ride along with the business. Each one has to be individually assigned to the buyer, and any contract with an anti-assignment clause needs the other party's written consent first, which typically takes 30–60 days per consent. A stock sale looks cleaner on paper: the entity keeps its contracts. But many contracts carry change-of-control clauses that demand permission anyway, most often triggered when more than 50% of the equity moves. Either way, the deal closes with one signature and then goes looking for a dozen more.

A business is a stack of promises

Strip away the trucks and the logo and a business is contracts: the lease, the customer agreements, the supplier terms, the equipment leases, the software licenses. CT Acquisitions pegs a typical small business at 20–50 contracts. Before you list, inventory the top 20 customer contracts by revenue and the top 20 vendor contracts by spend, and read each for assignment and change-of-control language. That's part of the 90–180 days of prep work covered in our 12-month preparation guide — and it's cheaper to find a landmine in month one than in escrow.

The legal wrapper decides everything

In an asset sale, the buyer's new entity signs fresh paper for everything; default contract law says contracts are freely assignable, but lawyers add anti-assignment clauses as boilerplate, so in practice most of your meaningful contracts need consent. In a stock sale the entity survives, but change-of-control clauses — triggered most commonly above 50% of equity, sometimes as low as 25–40% — put the counterparty back in the loop. Sometimes the contract review, not the tax math, picks the structure: a business with one monster anti-assignment contract leans toward a stock sale; one with broad change-of-control clauses leans the other way.

Default law actually starts on your side: as Clark Wilson's primer notes, contracts are freely assignable unless a statute, public policy, a personal-service character, or an increased burden on the counterparty says otherwise. The catch is that the exceptions got written into nearly everything as boilerplate decades ago, so the default rarely survives contact with a real contract file. Two more wrinkles worth knowing: in a share sale the seller's entity usually isn't released from old obligations just because the equity moved, and even after a valid assignment the original seller often remains secondarily liable unless the counterparty expressly lets them go — which is why purchase agreements carry indemnities running both directions.

The consent clock

Consents are a parallel workstream that should start the day the LOI is signed, not the week before closing. Axial's dead-deal data shows 25.3% of broken LOIs die in diligence — and slow consents are a classic way to burn exclusivity days you don't have.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Ray's commercial cleaning company does $900,000 in revenue with $170,000 SDE, asking $170,000 × 2.4 = $408,000 — a touch over the 2.30× market average. His anchor account, a medical-office group, is 28% of revenue: $252,000, which at a 30% margin carries $75,600 of SDE. That one contract represents $75,600 × 2.4 = $181,440 — about 44% of the price — hanging on one consent signature.

The contract has an anti-assignment clause. Rather than gamble the closing date, the parties close on time with 15% of the price — $61,200 — in a consent escrow. The medical group signs on day 41, the escrow releases, and nobody had to renegotiate anything in a panic.

When the counterparty says no

A consent request hands the other side leverage, and some use it: counterparties can demand concessions or fees as the price of a signature. Landlords are the classic case — assignment fees typically run $500 to $5,000, per Morgan & Westfield, and the lease has its own rulebook (see what happens to the lease).

If a customer balks, work the options ladder: renegotiate the contract; have the seller subcontract the work to the buyer as a bridge; terminate and reissue fresh paper in the buyer's name; carve the contract out of the deal with a price cut; or close with a consent escrow like Ray's. What you never do is assign it anyway — that hands the customer a termination right and the buyer an indemnity claim. And the buyer never contacts your customers unilaterally; that's how confidential sales stop being confidential (our guide on keeping a sale quiet applies to customers too).

Timing the asks is its own negotiation. Sellers prefer to wait until the purchase agreement is signed before approaching anyone, so a dead deal doesn't leave a trail of customers who know the business was for sale. Buyers want consents started at the LOI, so a slow landlord doesn't burn their exclusivity window. The usual compromise: rank the contracts by value, start the slow ones (landlord, franchisor, government) immediately after the LOI with carefully worded requests, and hold the routine customer consents until the deal is definitively happening. Every request should arrive with a one-page story — who the buyer is, why service won't change — because a consent request with no context reads like bad news, and counterparties price bad news.

The contracts that bite hardest

Five categories cause most of the pain. The lease, because the business usually can't operate anywhere else. The franchise agreement, where the franchisor runs its own approval gauntlet (see franchise resales). Government contracts, which can't be assigned at all — they're novated under FAR Subpart 42.12, a 3–6 month federal process. Equipment leases and software licenses, which hide anti-assignment language nobody has read since signing. And licenses and permits, which mostly don't transfer, period — a liquor license is a fresh application, not an assignment. For the broader question of what conveys at all, see what's included in the sale.

Frequently asked questions

Do contracts automatically transfer when a business is sold?

In an asset sale, no — each is assigned individually, and anti-assignment clauses require written consent. In a stock sale the entity keeps its contracts, but change-of-control clauses (most commonly triggered above 50% of equity) often require permission anyway.

What is an anti-assignment clause?

Boilerplate saying the contract can't move to a new party without written consent. Expect 30–60 days per customer or vendor consent, 60–90 for a landlord, and 3–6 months for a government novation.

What if a customer refuses to consent?

Renegotiate, bridge via subcontract, terminate-and-reissue, carve out with a price cut, or close with a consent escrow. Never assign anyway, and never let the buyer approach your customers on their own.
