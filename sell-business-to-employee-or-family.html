Can I Sell My Business to an Employee or Family Member?

By The Broker Index Team · November 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — selling to your longtime employee or your kid is legal, common, and often the smoothest exit there is. You skip the blind listing, the buyer screening, and the 155-day median search for a stranger with money. But insider sales trade those headaches for three new ones: there's no market to check your price against, the buyer usually has no cash, and if the buyer shares your last name, the IRS pulls up a chair. All three are solvable. Here's how.

Why insider sales are different

When strangers negotiate, the market sets the price. When Rosa sells to her crew lead, nobody knows what the business is "really" worth — which is exactly the situation where sellers get underpaid and family resentments get minted. So the first rule: get an independent valuation, not a handshake number. If the deal uses SBA financing you'll need one anyway — the SBA requires an independent business valuation on every financed change of ownership. For context on what the market pays, the BizBuySell Insight Report puts the Q3 2026 median sale at $350,000 and the average sub-$1M multiple at 2.30× SDE. Your valuation should land in defensible distance of that math, whoever the buyer is.

The money problem, and the three ways around it

Employees who could write a check for your business generally already own one. Three structures bridge the gap. First, the SBA 7(a) acquisition loan: the bank funds up to 90%, the buyer injects 10% — and a seller note on full standby can cover up to half that injection, so your employee can close with roughly 5% cash while the lender wants 1.25× coverage on documented actuals. Second, straight seller financing — you become the bank, with all the default risk that implies. Third, a gradual equity sale: the buyer purchases tranches over years, out of the profits the business pays them. Slowest for you, gentlest on them.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Rosa's landscaping company runs $160,000 of SDE; the valuation comes in at 2.5×, so the price is $400,000. Her crew lead Mateo buys it with an SBA 7(a) loan: the bank funds 90% — $360,000 at 10% over 10 years, which is $4,757/month, or $57,089/year.

The 10% injection is $40,000: Mateo brings $20,000 cash and Rosa carries a $20,000 seller note on full standby (no payments while the SBA loan is outstanding). Coverage math: $160,000 SDE minus a $60,000 manager-level salary for Mateo leaves $100,000 to service $57,089 of debt — a 1.75 DSCR, comfortably over the 1.25 benchmark. Rosa wires out of closing with roughly $380,000 — 95% of her price — and a small note she expects to collect later.

A word on the third structure, since it's the quiet workhorse of employee succession: in a gradual equity sale, the employee buys 10% or 20% tranches over several years, often funded by profit distributions and a modest salary bump they reinvest. It costs you speed and keeps you on the hook for the business's performance, but it lets you test the successor with real ownership stakes before the big tranche moves, and it gives a buyer with thin savings a runway no bank would offer. Many owners pair it with a put-call agreement — a pre-agreed price formula for the remaining equity — so nobody has to renegotiate value every year over the break-room coffee.

Family deals: the IRS is the third party at the table

Sell to your daughter at half price and the discount isn't generosity — it's a gift, in the technical, form-filing sense. Anything below fair market value counts. For 2026, the annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient, and the lifetime exemption is $15 million per person ($30 million per couple) — so very few family transfers owe actual tax, but transfers above the exclusion still require a Form 709 gift-tax return, built on that independent valuation.

If you carry a note for a family buyer, it has to charge at least the applicable federal rate or the foregone interest becomes a gift too. Under Rev. Rul. 2026-21, the November 2026 AFRs are 4.67% for notes up to 3 years, 5.08% for 3–9 years, and 5.52% beyond 9. And an installment sale spreads your capital gain across the payment years — though depreciation recapture lands in year one regardless; the details live in our guide to taxes when selling.

Go in with clear eyes

The classic research on family succession, from John Ward, found that only about 30% of family businesses survive through the second generation, and 10–15% through the third. The number isn't an argument against selling to your kid; it's an argument for selling to them the way you'd sell to a stranger — real valuation, real loan, real transition plan. An heir who buys the business tends to run it like an owner. An heir who's handed it tends to run it like an inheritance.

On transitions: SBA rules now let a seller stay on under a consulting agreement for up to 24 months after a financed sale, double the old window. Plan to use some of it — our transition-period guide covers what's normal. Nine years of watching you work is not the same as nine weeks of running payroll alone.

Do you still need a broker?

Half a broker's job is finding and screening buyers. In an insider sale that half is done, and many brokers will quote a reduced fee to run the rest: process quarterback, valuation defense, deal hygiene, keeping everyone cordial at Thanksgiving. What's non-negotiable, broker or not: the independent valuation, an attorney on the purchase agreement, and a CPA on the structure. Insider deals fail less from bad faith than from skipped paperwork — the SDE recast, the note terms, the gift-tax return someone forgot existed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just give the business to my son or daughter?

You can, but any transfer below fair market value is a gift: $19,000 annual exclusion, $15M lifetime exemption in 2026, Form 709 filing above the exclusion, and an independent valuation to support the numbers. A family note must charge at least the applicable federal rate.

Can an employee buy my business with no money down?

Close to it. SBA 7(a) requires a 10% injection, but your standby seller note can cover half, so the employee closes with about 5% cash — if documented cash flow covers the loan at roughly 1.25×.

Do I still need a broker?

Not the buyer-finding half. Many brokers take insider deals at reduced fees to run process and valuation defense. The valuation, attorney, and CPA are the parts you can't skip.

This article is general information, not tax or legal advice — bring your CPA and attorney in early, especially on family transfers.
