What Happens to the Lease When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Your lease doesn't follow the business the way the espresso machine does. Nearly every commercial lease requires the landlord's written consent before you can assign it to a buyer, and that consent is a process: expect 60 to 90 days, a fee that typically lands between $2,000 and $5,000, and a hard look at your buyer's financials. Handle it early and it's paperwork. Handle it late and the landlord is effectively a silent partner in your sale — one who can stall the deal, per BizBuySell's landlord guide, "right up until you are in escrow."

Why the landlord holds real cards

An assignment clause sounds like a formality until you read it. Most say consent "shall not be unreasonably withheld," which is comfort of the lawyerly kind: landlords can still slow-walk, demand a stronger guarantee, or use your request as an opening to renegotiate rent. Most state laws do limit refusal without good reason, but "good reason" includes a buyer with thin credit, no industry experience, or a business plan the landlord doesn't like. Your buyer isn't just being vetted by the bank — they're being vetted by the building.

The practical move, straight from the landlord playbook: tell your landlord you intend to sell before diligence starts. You don't need a buyer in hand to ask how they'd handle an assignment. Sellers consistently get better outcomes raising it early than springing a signed purchase agreement on a landlord who's hearing about the sale for the first time. (For the broader disclosure schedule — who to tell and when — see who should you tell that you're selling.)

The guarantee trap

Here's the part that bites a year later. Many assignments transfer the lease but don't release the original tenant. You sell, you move to the lake, the buyer misses rent in month nine — and the landlord's lawyer calls you, because your name and your personal guarantee are still on the paper. Per Morgan & Westfield's lease guidance, assignment fees themselves run $500 to $5,000, but staying on as guarantor is the expensive part. Negotiate a full release at assignment, or at minimum a sunset: your guarantee dies after the buyer makes, say, twelve clean payments. Landlords won't volunteer this. Ask.

Your rent is priced into your business — literally

Buyers don't just read the lease for the term. They read the rent, because every dollar of rent above market is a dollar off your earnings, multiplied. The restaurant benchmark makes it concrete: the National Restaurant Association's 2024 medians put occupancy cost at 5.7% of sales for full-service operators and 5.2% for limited-service (urban markets run closer to 6.0%).

Worked example (hypothetical)

Nina runs a bistro doing $1.2M in sales. Her rent works out to 7.2% of sales — $86,400 a year — against the 5.7% full-service median, which would be $68,400. That's an $18,000 annual rent gap.

Buyers price off earnings, and the gap comes straight out of them. At the Q3 2026 average multiple of 2.3× for sub-$1M businesses, $18,000 of excess rent costs $18,000 × 2.3 = $41,400 of sale price. Nina can't fix the lease overnight, but she can try to renegotiate before listing, document any percentage-rent offsets, or at least walk into pricing conversations knowing exactly what the lease is costing her.

The SBA's lease rule can kill a financeable deal

If your buyer is bringing SBA money — and most Main Street buyers are — the lease has a hard requirement: per SBA standard operating procedure, the lease term including renewal options must run at least as long as the loan, which on a business acquisition means up to 10 years (Starfield & Smith covers the rule's mechanics). A business on a two-year lease with no options isn't a smaller loan — it's frequently no loan. Before you list, check your remaining term plus options against a 10-year ruler, and if you're short, negotiate options now, while you have leverage and the landlord doesn't know you're leaving.

Month-to-month tenants, this means you. The flexibility you've enjoyed is a discount on your sale price.

Four ways a lease moves — and when each applies

Assignment is the standard: the buyer steps into your lease on its existing terms, with landlord consent. A new lease is sometimes better for everyone — the buyer gets the 10-year term the SBA wants, the landlord gets a fresh credit decision, and you get a clean exit with no lingering guarantee. Subletting is unusual in a business sale and most landlords prohibit it anyway. And if the seller owns the building, the lease gets written fresh as part of the deal — put the rent in writing at the same number used in the earnings the price was based on, because buyers and lenders will check (and if the buyer might purchase the building too, that's its own analysis).

Whichever route, the lease belongs on your document checklist from day one, and the consent workstream should start at the letter of intent, not at closing week. Lease consent is one of the classic third-party approvals that don't transfer automatically — and it's usually the slowest one.

A ninety-day lease plan for sellers

Three months before listing, pull the lease and read the assignment clause, the remaining term, the options, and your guarantee language. Two months out, have the early conversation with the landlord and ask what their consent process looks like — who reviews buyer financials, how long it takes, what the fee is. One month out, decide whether to negotiate fresh options now, while you're a paying tenant with alternatives rather than a seller with a deadline. The whole exercise costs an afternoon and routinely saves the six weeks of late-stage scrambling that kills momentum with a buyer who has other listings to look at. Deals don't usually die because the landlord says no; they die because the landlord was asked last.

Frequently asked questions

Does my lease transfer automatically when I sell?

No. You'll need the landlord's written consent to assign it. Budget 60–90 days, a $2,000–$5,000 fee, and a review of your buyer's financials. Raise it with your landlord early — before diligence, not during.

Do I stay liable after the sale?

Often, unless you negotiate a release. Assignments frequently leave your guarantee in place if the buyer defaults. Push for a full release or a sunset after a run of clean payments.

How much lease term does a buyer need?

Three-plus years with options to satisfy most buyers; term-plus-options matching the loan length — up to 10 years — to satisfy an SBA lender. Short leases shrink your buyer pool and your price.
