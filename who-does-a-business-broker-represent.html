Does a Business Broker Represent the Buyer or the Seller?

By The Broker Index Team · November 16, 2026 · 8 min read

The seller, almost always. The broker signed a listing agreement with the seller, and the broker gets paid by the seller — typically 10–12% of the price, at closing. In agency terms, the seller is the client: owed loyalty, confidentiality, and advice. The buyer is the customer: owed honesty and fair dealing, but not advocacy, not advice, and definitely not an opinion on whether the price is fair. If you're buying, that one sentence should reorganize how you read every email the broker sends you.

Follow the signature, then the check

Business brokerage fees make the loyalty structure plain. Per BizBuySell's fee guide, sellers typically pay 10–15% on deals up to about $1M, with step-downs above — say 10% on the first million and 8% over — and flat fees are common under $100K. The buyer pays the broker nothing. Our fees article has the full menu. There's a floor under everyone: even a pure seller's broker owes the buyer fair dealing, meaning no lies and no hidden material facts. But screening buyers, which the broker does energetically, is done for the seller — that's what the proof-of-funds dance in vetting serious buyers is about.

Dual agency: the industry's open secret

Here's the wrinkle: in business sales, buyers usually come straight to the listing broker, because there's no MLS culture pushing them to a separate agent — the marketplaces funnel every inquiry to whoever posted the listing. So the same broker ends up handling both sides. One longtime California intermediary, in a BizBen practitioner discussion, put his own career share at roughly 99% dual agency. That's one broker's self-report, not a census, but nobody in the thread found it shocking.

A disclosed dual agent can still earn their fee: running the process, moving paperwork, keeping the deal alive. What they legally can't do is advocate on price for either side or reveal either side's walk-away number. A referee, not a coach. Useful — as long as neither side mistakes the referee for their coach.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Why does every listing seem to close in-house? A Business Brokerage Press survey reported via Transworld found 52% of brokers said 15% or fewer of their deals were co-brokered — and 38% said under 5%. The fee math explains it. On a $350,000 sale (the Q3 2026 median) at 10%, the fee is $35,000. Co-broked 50/50, each office takes $17,500. Keep both sides in-house and the take doubles.

The price incentive is quieter but real. On a $400,000 listing with a $380,000 offer on the table, holding out for full ask earns the seller another $18,000 — but earns the broker only $2,000 more, while risking the entire $40,000 fee if the deal dies. For brokers, closing beats maximizing. In both directions.

The state-law patchwork

Agency rules ride on licensing law, and licensing is a patchwork. In the roughly 17 states that require a real estate license to broker business sales — the map in our real-estate-agent guide, from Business Brokerage Press — real estate agency law comes along, with its disclosure forms. Per HomeLight, eight states ban real-estate dual agency outright: Alaska, Colorado, Florida, Kansas, Maryland, Texas, Vermont, and Wyoming. Florida and Colorado route both-sides deals through a neutral "transaction broker"; Texas uses an intermediary structure. And in states with no licensing requirement at all, there may be no mandatory disclosure form whatsoever. The fix costs one email: ask, in writing, "Who do you represent in this transaction?"

Buying? Act like the broker works for the seller — because they do

What does "fair dealing" buy you as a buyer, concretely? The broker can't lie to you about material facts, can't forge the financials, and can't hide a known problem you've directly asked about. What it doesn't buy: they won't tell you the seller would take less, won't point out the weak spots in the CIM, and won't warn you that the asking price is a multiple nobody's paid in that industry since 2021. Those omissions aren't misconduct. They're the job description.

So act accordingly. Never tell the listing broker your maximum price; anything you share can and will be used in negotiation, because passing it along is literally their job. Bring your own attorney and CPA, and run your own due diligence — the broker's package is marketing, not verification. If you want real representation, true buy-side engagements run a flat $5,000–$25,000 per BizBuySell, half at signing and half at closing. And watch for the "buyer's broker" who's paid out of the listing broker's commission split. That's a dual agent with better marketing.

Selling? The loyalty is yours — make sure you're getting it

Ask two questions in writing before you sign: will you co-broke this listing, and at what split? A broker who refuses co-brokering is narrowing your buyer pool to protect their fee. Then ask how direct buyers will be handled — dual agency with your written consent, or a designated-agent setup where someone else in the office works the buyer. Our eight questions and trust guide go deeper. None of this makes brokers villains. It makes them agents, and agents follow the agreement — so read yours. The sellers who get burned aren't the ones with aggressive brokers; they're the ones who never asked what the agreement actually said until the tail-fee invoice arrived.

Frequently asked questions

Does the broker represent the buyer or the seller?

The seller, almost always — that's who signed the listing agreement and pays the 10–12% fee. Buyers get honesty and fair dealing, not advocacy.

Can one broker represent both sides?

Usually yes, as a disclosed dual agent, and in practice it's extremely common because buyers contact the listing broker directly. A dual agent runs process but can't advocate price or share confidential limits. Eight states ban the real-estate version outright, and a few others route both-sides deals through a neutral transaction broker or intermediary instead.

Should I hire my own broker when buying?

A true buy-side engagement (flat $5K–$25K) can be worth it on a serious search. A "free" buyer's broker paid from the listing split is a dual agent. Either way, your attorney and CPA are the non-negotiables.
