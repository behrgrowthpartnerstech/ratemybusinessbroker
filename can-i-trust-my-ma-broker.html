How Do I Know If I Can Trust My M&A Broker? 12 Red Flags

By The Broker Index Team · September 18, 2026 · 8 min read

You've signed with a broker — or you're about to — and something feels slightly off. Maybe the valuation seemed a little too good, or the updates stopped coming, or the fee conversation keeps sliding sideways. Trust your instinct enough to check it. Here are twelve red flags, assembled from industry checklists, regulator guidance, and the patterns that show up in low-scoring reviews on The Broker Index — plus what to do when you spot one.

Before you sign

1. A large upfront fee, out of proportion to your deal

Retainers exist in legitimate mid-market M&A, but for a main-street business a heavy upfront "marketing" or "valuation" fee is the classic misalignment: the broker gets paid whether or not you ever close. BizBuySell's red-flags guide lists it first for a reason.

2. A guaranteed price or timeline

Markets don't offer guarantees, so anyone selling one is selling something else. Compare with the earnings-claim tactics the FTC prosecutes in bogus business-opportunity schemes — "guaranteed" is the load-bearing word in most of them.

3. A valuation dramatically above every other opinion

If three professionals say $1.2M and one says $2.5M, the outlier isn't a genius — they're buying your listing. You'll pay for the flattery later, in price cuts and wasted months.

4. Pressure to sign today, and resistance to attorney review

A professional expects your lawyer to read the engagement agreement and is comfortable waiting. Rushing you past review is not enthusiasm; it's a strategy.

5. No verifiable credentials or licensing

Check whether your state requires business brokers to hold a real-estate license, and whether the broker carries industry credentials like the CBI from the IBBA. Absence isn't proof of anything — but combined with other flags, it matters.

Once you're engaged

6. Silence

No inquiry reports, no update cadence, no returned calls. The listing-mill model depends on clients who don't ask questions. Set a reporting rhythm in writing and treat sustained silence as data.

7. A leaky "confidential" process

If your employees, competitors, or landlord learn your business is for sale from someone other than you, the process has already cost you money. Ask to see exactly what marketing materials say and who has signed NDAs.

8. Phantom buyers

"We have several very interested parties" — but no NDAs signed, no names, no offers, month after month. Ask for specifics you can verify: number of inquiries, number of NDAs, number of buyer calls.

9. Undisclosed dual agency

A broker representing both sides of your deal isn't automatically corrupt — but it must be disclosed, in writing, and you should understand exactly whose interests they're paid to serve at each stage.

10. Fee terms that mutate mid-deal

New charges appearing after signature — "document fees," "buyer-vetting fees" — are a contract problem and a character problem at once.

11. Hostility to verification

Whether you're the buyer asking for source financials or the seller asking who the buyer is, a trustworthy intermediary facilitates verification. Obstruction of diligence is the strongest single predictor of a deal you'll regret — it's the through-line in fraud cases like the fake-financials lawsuit we covered here.

12. Their past clients go quiet

When you ask references the pointed questions — "would you use them again?" — listen for the pause. And check reviews the broker doesn't curate.

What to do if you're seeing these flags

  • Document everything now — emails, call notes, dates, promised vs. delivered. If things go wrong, contemporaneous records are your leverage.
  • Ask the direct question in writing. "How many NDAs have been signed on my listing?" forces specificity and creates a paper trail.
  • Reread your engagement letter for its termination clause, tail period, and fee triggers — with a lawyer if real money is at stake.
  • Get a second opinion from another intermediary or your accountant; a thirty-minute call recalibrates fast.
  • Leave a review. Whatever happens with your deal, your experience is exactly the information the next seller needs. Rate your broker here — it takes five minutes.
