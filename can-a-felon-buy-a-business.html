Can a Felon Buy a Business? SBA Loans, Licenses, and the Rules in 2026

By The Broker Index Team · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes. No federal law bars you from owning a business after a conviction — none ever has — and since May 30, 2024, the SBA's Criminal Justice Reviews final rule removed criminal-history questions from 7(a) and 504 applications entirely and ended the old parole-and-probation ineligibility. What's left on the federal side is narrow: you can't be currently incarcerated, serving a sentence after a guilty adjudication, or under indictment for a felony or a financial-misconduct or false-statement crime, and a prior default on federal debt still bites. The real obstacles now live elsewhere: licensing boards, franchisor checks, and ordinary lender math.

What changed in 2024

The SBA's announcement framed the stakes: roughly 4 million Americans on parole or probation, about 27% unemployment among the formerly incarcerated, and research showing entrepreneurship cuts recidivism by up to 30% for unemployed returning citizens. The mechanics, per the Coleman Report: Form 1919's question now asks only about present status — currently incarcerated, serving a sentence, or under indictment for the listed crimes. Yes means ineligible, no waiver. Everything before today is off the form. The current SOP 50 10 8 carries the same short list, per Starfield Smith's eligibility summary, alongside the old standbys: no delinquent federal debt, no prior loss to the government, standard fraud checks.

The scale of who this touches is easy to underestimate. The Clean Slate Initiative's data model counts more than 71 million Americans with a state conviction or arrest record — the framing their CEO uses is one in three adults. That's not a niche of buyers. That's the buyer pool.

Eligible isn't the same as approved

The rule change fixed eligibility. Underwriting is still a lender's call, made case by case, and a lender who learns the history elsewhere can still weigh it. The file that wins is the standard file: a credit score around 680, no delinquent federal debt (the CAIVRS database checks), a documented down payment, and a business whose cash flow covers the loan at the usual 1.25 coverage ratio. In other words, the same file everyone else needs — which is precisely the point of the rule.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Marcus, released six years ago and now an operations manager, buys a $300,000 lawn-care business. Total project: $335,000 ($300K + $20K working capital + $15K closing costs). His 10% injection: $33,500. Loan: $301,500 at 10% over 10 years = $3,984 a month, or $47,812 a year.

The business produces $120,000 of SDE. Minus the $55,000 Marcus needs to live: $65,000 available for debt service. Coverage: $65,000 ÷ $47,812 = 1.36 — past the bar. The underwriter reads income, coverage, credit, and injection. The form no longer asks about anything else.

Seller financing deserves its own line here, because it's the lane with no committee. A retiring owner carrying a note answers to nobody's underwriting manual — they size you up across a table, and six years of W-2s plus a serious down payment often reads better in person than it scores on paper. The trade is that you're asking one individual to take the risk a bank declined to price, so expect a shorter note, a higher rate, and a personal guarantee with teeth. Fair enough. The lane exists, and people drive in it every week.

Licenses: where the record still bites

Most Main Street businesses — landscaping, cleaning, e-commerce, restaurants in most states — need no professional license that checks records. But flat statutory bars exist, and they're specific. Minnesota, to take one linked example, won't issue a new retail liquor license to anyone with a felony conviction within five years of the application. Childcare, healthcare, private security, and cannabis all run background checks somewhere in the chain. Two practical notes: licenses generally don't transfer with the business anyway — you're applying fresh — and the time to confirm with the agency is before the letter of intent, as part of due diligence, not during closing week.

Franchisors and landlords: the discretionary gates

A franchise resale needs franchisor approval, and franchisors run background checks. A lease assignment needs the landlord's consent, and some landlords screen. These gates are discretionary, which cuts both ways: no statute bars you, and no statute protects you. The winning move is arriving with the story before the background check tells it for you — six years of steady work, the licenses you do hold, the lender already committed. Your broker can stage-manage those introductions; just remember who the listing broker works for when the conversation turns to price.

The playbook

First, screen industries before you fall in love: pick a target where no licensing board holds a veto, or confirm the board's rules in writing early. Second, build the standard file — credit near 680, documented down payment, clean federal-debt record — because the file is where the decision actually happens; our low-cash guide covers stretching the down payment legally. Third, look into expungement or record sealing before you start shopping; a sealed record simplifies every discretionary gate at once, and the Clean Slate movement exists precisely because most eligible people never file. Fourth, if bank financing stalls anyway, seller financing is the fallback lane — the seller decides for themselves, and our seller-financing guide shows what they'll want to see.

One caveat worth repeating: these rules changed recently and can change again, and state licensing law is its own maze. This article is general information, not legal advice — for a specific license or a specific record, twenty minutes with an attorney beats a month of forum threads.

Frequently asked questions

Can a felon get an SBA loan?

Generally yes, since May 30, 2024. The application no longer asks about criminal history; the bars are current incarceration, serving a sentence, or indictment for a felony or financial-misconduct crime. Lenders still underwrite credit and cash flow normally.

Can a felon get a business license?

Usually — most small businesses need none. Watch the exceptions: liquor (Minnesota bars new licenses within five years of a felony), childcare, healthcare, security, cannabis. Confirm with the agency before the LOI.

Does a felony show up when buying a business?

Not on the SBA form anymore. Franchisors, some landlords, and licensing boards still check — so bring the story to them first.
