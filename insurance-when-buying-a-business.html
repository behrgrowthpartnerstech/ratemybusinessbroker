What Insurance Do You Need When You Buy a Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 24, 2026 · 9 min read

Plan on all of it being yours, from scratch, effective the minute you own the place. As BizBuySell's Learning Center puts it: "Most small business insurance policies don't transfer automatically to new owners." So the buyer's job is to bind general liability (or a BOP), workers comp, commercial auto, and property coverage by closing — not the week after — while pulling 3–5 years of the seller's loss runs during diligence and settling who pays for tail coverage on any claims-made policy. Here's the stack, with real prices.

The policies stay behind. The risk doesn't.

In an asset sale — the standard Main Street structure — the seller's legal entity is the named insured, and that entity stops operating at closing. The policies get cancelled, sometimes with a partial refund to the seller, sometimes with charges. Formal novation of a policy to a new owner is rare, and a handful of states route insurance-business transfers through the state insurance department. None of that helps you at 9 a.m. on day one, when a customer slips on your newly purchased floor. Coverage has to be bound and effective at the moment of transfer, which makes insurance a closing-day checklist item, right next to the wire confirmations.

The day-one stack, with actual prices

Median monthly costs from Insureon's data — drawn from roughly 100,000 small-business customers: general liability $45 ($538/yr at $1M/$2M limits), workers comp $54, a business owner's policy $83 ($990/yr — GL plus property bundled, usually the better buy), umbrella $86, commercial property $108. Three notes on fit: workers comp is state law once you have employees, not a judgment call; your landlord will demand a certificate of insurance before consenting to the lease assignment; and any titled vehicle needs commercial auto, priced separately by carrier.

The lender has opinions too

If you're financing through the SBA, insurance stops being optional paperwork. Under SOP 50 10 8, hazard insurance is mandatory on loans over $50,000 — in Lenders Cooperative's summary, "loans cannot be approved without it," a tightening from the prior SOP's discretion. Flood insurance applies when the property sits in a special flood hazard area. And when the whole business depends on one person, lenders generally require term life coverage, collaterally assigned to the bank — the amount and structure are the lender's call, so ask early. The practical warning inside all of this: insurance underwriting takes weeks, not days, and your SBA timeline won't wait for a slow application you started late.

Loss runs: diligence that cuts both ways

Loss runs — the carrier's claim history reports — are the business's permanent record. Buyers request 3–5 years of them per BizBuySell, because the pattern tells you things the P&L won't: the slip-and-falls, the vehicle claims, the workers comp history that will set your premiums. It cuts the other way too. If the seller has been running bare — no workers comp, no umbrella — their expenses are understated, which means their SDE is overstated. Underinsurance is an add-back in reverse, and you should re-price for it. Sellers reading this a year before listing: buy the missing policies now, so the expense is in the books and priced, instead of becoming a buyer's discovery and a renegotiation.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Grace is buying at the Q3 2026 medians: price $350,000, SDE $156,000, multiple 2.30×. Diligence shows the seller never carried workers comp — a $4,800/yr policy for this payroll. Restated SDE: $151,200. Price impact at the market multiple: $4,800 × 2.30 = $11,040 off the ask, and that's a negotiation she can document line by line.

Her own day-one stack: BOP $990 + workers comp $648 + umbrella $1,032 ≈ $2,670 a year — about 1.7% of SDE. The cheapest fix in this entire transaction.

Tail coverage: the seller's last policy

Liability policies come in two flavors. Occurrence policies (most general liability) cover incidents that happened while the policy was active, whenever the claim arrives. Claims-made policies (most professional liability) only respond to claims filed while coverage is live — so when the seller cancels at closing, claims from their ownership years can land on them personally. The fix is tail coverage, formally an extended reporting period: per biBerk, a one-time premium around 100–200% of the final annual premium, bought in a strict window after the policy ends, for terms of 1–5 years or unlimited. Who pays is a negotiation — settle it in the purchase agreement alongside the holdback terms, because "we'll sort it out later" is how it becomes the buyer's problem.

The one-week version

During diligence: request 3–5 years of loss runs, confirm which policies are claims-made, and get an agent quoting your stack against the seller's current coverage — the quote doubles as a diligence document. In the LOI-to-closing stretch: bind GL/BOP, workers comp, auto, and property effective at closing; deliver the certificate to the landlord and the lender; nail down tail payment in the APA. After closing: calendar the renewals and re-shop in year one, because the first-year premiums priced your predecessor's history, not yours. It all belongs in the same folder as the rest of the deal paperwork.

And a quiet broker test: ask the broker handling your deal when insurance should be bound. The good ones answer "the day you close, and we'll have the COI to the landlord the week before." The ones who say "oh, after closing is fine" have just told you how they run the rest of the file — which is what the reviews on our rating pages exist to catch.

Frequently asked questions

Does insurance transfer when you buy a business?

Almost never. The seller's entity is the named insured and it stops operating; policies cancel, novation is rare. Bind your own coverage effective at closing.

How much does small business insurance cost?

Insureon medians: GL $45/mo, workers comp $54, BOP $83, umbrella $86, property $108. A typical starter stack runs about $2,670 a year.

Who pays for tail coverage?

Whoever the APA says. It's a one-time 100–200% of the final annual premium on claims-made policies, protecting the seller's ownership years — negotiate it before closing, in writing.
