Business Broker vs. M&A Advisor: Which One Do You Need to Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 16, 2026 · 9 min read

A business broker and an M&A advisor both sell companies, but they're different machines for different weight classes. A broker lists Main Street businesses — restaurants, shops, service companies, generally under $1–2M — on marketplaces, prices them off SDE, and earns a success fee around 10–12%. An M&A advisor sells lower-middle-market companies by running a competitive process: direct outreach to strategic buyers and private equity, confidential auctions, pricing off EBITDA, paid by retainer plus a scaled success fee. Below roughly $700K of earnings, the broker is usually right. Above $1M of EBITDA, the advisor usually is. In between sits a crossover zone where the choice can swing your net proceeds by six figures — in either direction.

What each one actually does all day

A broker's process: value the business, build a confidential listing, post to marketplaces like BizBuySell, field inquiries, screen buyers, and shepherd one deal to closing. It's a volume trade — reactive by design, and cost-effective because marketplace buyers come to the listing. Fees run 8–15% under $1M with minimums of $10–25K; many shops use 10% as the anchor (the full fee landscape is in how brokers get paid).

An advisor's process: build a buyer list of 50–200 strategics and funds, write a proper CIM, run outreach in waves, collect indications of interest, stage management meetings, and force buyers to bid against each other on price and terms. That machinery costs money up front — retainers of $5–50K, usually credited against the success fee — and minimum success fees of $35–50K make small deals uneconomic. One more structural difference: above certain thresholds securities law enters the room. The 2023 federal M&A broker exemption (effective March 30, 2023) lets unregistered intermediaries handle private company sales where the target is under $25M EBITDA or $250M revenue and the buyer takes control, per Greenberg Traurig's analysis — but states aren't preempted, and bigger or minority-stake deals may need a registered broker-dealer. You can check registrations on FINRA BrokerCheck.

The fee formulas, decoded

Advisors typically quote a Lehman variant. Classic Lehman is 5/4/3/2/1; the modern standard is Double Lehman: 10% of the first million, 8% of the second, 6% of the third, 4% of the fourth, 2% above. A warning about sloppy math in the wild: BizQuest's fee guide cites $300,000 as the Double Lehman fee on a $3M sale. Run it yourself: $100K + $80K + $60K = $240,000, an 8% effective rate. If a published guide can fumble the formula, so can an engagement letter — do your own arithmetic before signing.

When the expensive one is cheaper

Worked example (hypothetical)

Elena's logistics company earns $1.1M of EBITDA — squarely in the crossover zone. A capable broker lists it and finds a buyer at 3.3×: $3,630,000, minus a flat 10% fee of $363,000, nets $3,267,000.

An M&A advisor runs a process instead: forty strategics and funds contacted, six indications, three final bids. Competitive tension lands the deal at 3.9×: $4,290,000. The Double Lehman fee: $100,000 + $80,000 + $60,000 + $40,000 + 2% × $290,000 = $285,800. Net: $4,004,200.

The advisor charged less in percentage terms and moved the multiple — Elena clears $737,200 more. That's the case for the auction at this size. The honest counterweight: below the zone, there may be no strategic buyers to auction, and the retainer plus $35–50K minimum fee just subtracts from a marketplace-driven outcome a broker would've reached anyway. The machine has to fit the deal.

How to choose in the crossover zone

Roughly $700K–$1M+ of EBITDA is where it gets genuinely close, and IBBA Market Pulse data explains why: multiples climb from about 2.0× on sub-$500K deals to 4.3× in the $5–50M bracket, and 83% of $5M+ deals attract three or more offers. The question is whether your company can summon that competition. Ask candidates for both roles the same things: the last five closed deals with sizes and multiples (then verify — see checking references), who the realistic buyers are by name, and exactly how the fee math works at three different price outcomes. An advisor who can't name likely strategic buyers for your company is a broker with a retainer. A broker who says "we'll see what the market says" about a $1M-EBITDA company is leaving an auction unrun.

Watch the engagement letter either way: tail periods, minimum fees, what happens on a structured offer with earnouts (does the fee hit paper value or cash received?), and termination rights. The fee formula gets the attention; the definitions around it move the money.

The hybrid cases, and the ones that fool people

Some situations blur the line and deserve a second look. A $600K-SDE business in a consolidating industry — HVAC, pest control, veterinary, MSPs — can punch above its weight class, because private equity roll-ups are actively buying at that size and a light auction among four known acquirers behaves like a lower-middle-market process. Conversely, a $1.2M-EBITDA business with heavy owner dependence or one dominant customer may draw zero strategic interest no matter who runs the process; an honest advisor will tell you that in the first meeting, and the dishonest one will take the retainer. Title matters less than you'd hope here: plenty of shops call themselves "M&A advisors" while running a listing-and-wait process at Double Lehman prices, which is the worst of both menus. The tell is always the buyer list. A real process starts with names; a listing starts with a login.

And if you're well under the zone — a $150K-SDE shop, say — skip the agonizing entirely. No advisor economics work at that size, marketplace buyers are plentiful, and your energy belongs on pricing the business right and keeping it confidential while it markets.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a business broker and an M&A advisor?

Brokers list Main Street businesses on marketplaces, price off SDE, and charge ~10–12% success fees. Advisors run competitive auctions for lower-middle-market companies, price off EBITDA, and charge retainers plus scaled fees like Double Lehman.

What is the Double Lehman formula?

10% of the first million, 8% of the second, 6% of the third, 4% of the fourth, 2% above. On $3M: $240,000 — not the $300,000 some guides misquote.

At what size do I need an advisor?

The crossover is roughly $700K–$1M+ EBITDA, where real buyer competition exists. Below it, broker economics usually win. Inside it, interview both with the same hard questions.
