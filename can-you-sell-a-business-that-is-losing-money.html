Can You Sell a Business That's Losing Money?

By The Broker Index Team · October 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes. Businesses that lose money on paper sell every month, for two reasons. First, many "losses" aren't losses: once you add back the owner's salary and the personal expenses run through the books, the real owner benefit is often solidly positive, and the business prices like any other. Second, even a genuine loss-maker owns things buyers want — customers, a lease, licenses, equipment, trained staff — and a going-concern sale captures their value in a way a liquidation auction never will. What you can't do is sell a loss-maker the lazy way. The story, the buyer pool, and the pricing method all change.

Step one: find out if you're actually losing money

Small-business P&Ls are built to minimize taxes, not to flatter the business. The industry's correction is seller's discretionary earnings — net profit plus owner compensation plus defensible add-backs. Run the recast before you conclude anything.

Worked example (hypothetical)

A shop reports a net loss of −$12,000. Add back the owner's salary of $60,000, plus documented add-backs — $13,000 of owner health insurance and vehicle, $7,400 of depreciation, $3,600 of one-time legal fees. Recast SDE: −12,000 + 60,000 + 13,000 + 7,400 + 3,600 = $72,000. At a cautious 2.0× multiple, that's a $144,000 business. Not a fortune, but a real sale — from a company whose tax return says it "loses money."

Separately, consider Carmen, whose genuinely struggling café still sold as a going concern for $168,000 — customers, lease, and equipment in place — versus the $74,500 her equipment was estimated to fetch at auction. Selling the business instead of its parts kept $93,500.

If the loss is real, the pricing floor is asset value

A business with genuinely negative earnings prices off its assets, and asset values come in three flavors appraisers use. Fair market value (FMV): what the assets fetch between willing parties with time to negotiate. Orderly liquidation value (OLV): a managed sell-off over months, typically 60–80% of FMV. Forced liquidation value (FLV): the auction gavel, typically 40–60% of FMV — and sometimes under 25%, with auctions running 60–90 days anyway. Every rung down the ladder is money gone, which is why "I'll just close and sell the equipment" is usually the most expensive plan available.

Typical ranges: OLV 60–80% of FMV, FLV 40–60% and sometimes below 25% — see Divestopedia on liquidation values.

Who actually buys loss-makers

Competitors are the best buyers: your revenue drops onto their existing overhead, so a business that loses money alone makes money bolted onto theirs. (Approach them carefully and under NDA — confidentiality matters most when the buyer is across the street.) Strategic buyers want something specific: your lease in a location they can't get, your licenses, your contracts. Turnaround operators buy cheap, cut hard, and know exactly what they're doing — expect aggressive terms and earnout-heavy structures. Asset buyers are the floor: they're paying OLV-ish prices, but privately and faster than an auction. Real sellers of struggling businesses, as the case studies at They Got Acquired keep showing, usually land with the first two groups.

Selling it well: the loss-maker playbook

Tell the truth fast. Buyers of struggling businesses have seen everything; a clear "here's why it loses money and here's the fix" builds more value than optimism. The classic sellable losses: an owner who checked out, one bad contract, a bloated lease, a missing sales function. The unsellable one: a dying market.

Price the turnaround, don't charge for it. You can't bill the buyer for profits they will create. What you can do is document the bridge — "remove this $40K contract and the business clears $28K" — and let the asset floor set your minimum.

Expect structure. All-cash offers for loss-makers are rare; expect seller financing or earnouts to carry part of the price. Take credit risk seriously — a struggling business sold to a struggling buyer comes back to you in worse shape.

Mind the lender gap. Banks don't finance negative cash flow, so your buyer pool is cash buyers and competitors with balance sheets. That shrinks the audience and lengthens the clock; if the business is still sliding, every quarter costs real money, which is the honest argument for deciding quickly rather than perfectly. (If you're weighing giving up entirely, read what happens if your business doesn't sell first — the options overlap.)

Hire a broker who has sold distress before. Positioning a loss-maker is a different craft from marketing a cash cow. Ask candidates for loss-maker comps, and check their reviews. A broker who overprices a struggling business doesn't just fail to sell it — they burn the months the business couldn't afford.

Timing: why loss-makers can't afford a slow sale

A profitable business can shrug off a long listing; a declining one can't, because the asset is melting while the process runs. Every quarter of losses shrinks working capital, ages the equipment, and tempts another key employee to leave — and buyers reprice all of it at the next diligence pass. This changes the strategy in two ways. First, price to move on day one rather than testing the market: the traditional "start high, come down slowly" approach burns the exact months you don't have. Second, go direct and parallel — have the broker approach the five most logical competitors and strategics in week one under NDA rather than waiting for marketplace inquiries to trickle in. A tight process with a realistic number routinely beats a leisurely one with a hopeful number, and for a loss-maker the gap between those outcomes is often the difference between a sale and a shutdown. If the trend line is the problem rather than the level, consider whether six months of focused fixes before listing — cutting the one bad contract, exiting the unprofitable location — would move you from "distressed" pricing to merely "underperforming" pricing. That jump is usually worth more than any negotiation tactic.

Frequently asked questions

Who buys businesses that are losing money?

Competitors, strategic buyers who want the lease or licenses, turnaround operators, and asset buyers — roughly in that order of price paid. All of them typically beat liquidation.

How is a money-losing business valued?

Recast first — many losses become positive SDE and price normally. Genuinely negative earnings price off assets: OLV at 60–80% of FMV, FLV at 40–60%, sometimes under 25%.

Is it better to sell a failing business or close it?

Sell, almost always. A going-concern sale captures customers, lease, and staff in the price; closing captures auction value for the equipment and nothing else. In our hypothetical, the difference was $93,500.
