Can I Get an SBA Loan to Buy a Business? The 2026 Rules

By The Broker Index Team · September 28, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes — buyers finance small-business purchases with SBA 7(a) loans every day, and in 2026 roughly 78% of buyers expect to. The honest version of the answer has three parts: the loan goes up to $5 million, you'll need about 10% of the deal in cash, and the business itself — not your resume, not the asking price — has to produce enough cash flow to cover the loan payments with room to spare. If it does, the SBA program is the cheapest large lever a first-time buyer will ever pull. If it doesn't, no amount of enthusiasm fixes the arithmetic.

How a 7(a) acquisition loan actually works

The SBA doesn't lend you money. A bank does, and the SBA guarantees most of it, which is why banks will fund a stranger buying a laundromat at all. The 7(a) program caps at $5 million, terms for business acquisitions run 10 years, and rates float with prime — loans over $350,000 cap at prime plus 3%, which with prime at 7% puts you near 10–10.25% today.

Your side of the table is the equity injection: at least 10% of total project cost. A seller note can cover part of it if the seller agrees to full standby — no payments while the standby period runs — but lenders like seeing your own cash at risk. On top of the 10%, budget for working capital, inventory, and closing costs, because the business needs to make payroll the Monday after you buy it.

The only ratio that matters: DSCR

Lenders test one thing above all: debt-service coverage — the cash the business generates, divided by the annual loan payments. The SBA's floor is 1.15; most credit committees want about 1.25. And "cash the business generates" means cash after paying you a market salary to run it, because you have to eat while you repay the bank.

This is why overpriced listings die quietly in underwriting. The seller can ask whatever they like; the lender runs the coverage math on the real earnings and hands back the number the deal supports. Sellers meet this ratio twice — once when pricing, once when their buyer's loan is approved or isn't.

Worked example (hypothetical)

An HVAC company earns $200,000 in SDE and is offered at $700,000 (3.5×). A buyer brings 10% cash ($70,000) and borrows $630,000 at 10.25% over 10 years. Payment ≈ $8,413/month = $100,956/year.

Cash available = $200,000 SDE − $65,000 owner replacement salary = $135,000. DSCR = 135,000 ÷ 100,956 = 1.34 — clears the bar, deal fundable.

Now reprice the same business at $800,000: the $720,000 loan costs about $115,380/year, and DSCR falls to 135,000 ÷ 115,380 = 1.17 — above the SBA floor, below what most lenders will actually approve. Same business, same buyer; the extra $100,000 of asking price is what killed it.

What changed in 2026

Lending got stricter, and sellers feel it in pricing. In BizBuySell's Q1 2026 survey, 45% of brokers cited tighter SBA underwriting as a drag on deals. Then the SBA's updated rulebook — SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 — added a notable requirement: on acquisitions of $3 million or more, lenders must obtain a quality of earnings report and use its adjusted numbers in the coverage math. We cover what that report checks in our QoE guide — the short version is that generous add-backs now get audited before the loan closes.

Two structural rules worth knowing before you draft an offer. Seller notes are welcome and can even count toward the injection on standby terms — our seller financing guide covers the seller's side of that. Earnouts are banned in SBA deals outright: the price must be fixed at closing, period.

Getting from application to closing

Finding the lender matters as much as qualifying. Banks vary enormously in SBA appetite — a "Preferred Lender" (PLP) can approve in-house instead of routing through the SBA, which saves weeks. The SBA's own Lender Match tool is the free starting point, and brokers who do SBA deals weekly will have two or three banks that already like their paperwork.

Expect 45–90 days from application to closing, partly overlapping due diligence. Your file will need three years of the business's tax returns, interim financials, your personal financial statement, and a plan showing you can actually run the thing. Buyers who show up with that package assembled are the ones brokers call serious — the LOI stage moves noticeably faster for them.

One more seller-side note, since plenty of owners read this page too: if your likely buyer needs SBA financing — and at Main Street size, four out of five do — then your business's SBA-fundability is your marketability. Clean books, defensible add-backs, and a price that survives the DSCR math aren't bureaucratic niceties. They're what make your listing sellable to most of the market. A broker who prices your business without running this math first is pricing it for a buyer who mostly doesn't exist — worth remembering when you're vetting who to hire.

Frequently asked questions

How much down payment do I need for an SBA loan to buy a business?

At least 10% of total project cost. Part can be a seller note on full standby, but lenders want real cash from you too. On a $700,000 deal, plan roughly $70,000 down plus working capital and closing costs.

What DSCR do SBA lenders require?

SBA's floor is 1.15; most lenders underwrite to about 1.25. The business's cash flow after a market salary for you must cover annual debt payments 1.25 times over — that math sets the real ceiling on price.

Can the seller finance part of an SBA deal?

Yes — seller notes are common and can count toward part of the equity injection on standby terms. Earnouts, though, are prohibited in SBA deals: the price must be fixed at closing.
