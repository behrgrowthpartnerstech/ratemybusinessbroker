How Long Do You Have to Stay After Selling Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Shorter than you fear, longer than zero. The standard package in a Main Street sale has three layers: an included training block — sample deal terms run around 125–150 hours across the first 90 days — then reasonable phone and email availability for 6 to 12 months, then optional consulting that the buyer pays for by the hour. Full-time, shoulder-to-shoulder presence usually lasts two to four weeks. And as of October 2026, SBA-financed deals allow the seller to consult for up to 24 months — double the old limit — though still strictly as a consultant, not an employee or owner.

Layer one: the training block the price includes

Purchase agreements spell this out in hours, not vibes. MidStreet's sample LOI language grants the buyer 150 on-site hours within three months of closing; BizBuySell's example terms run 125 hours over the same stretch. The shape is always a taper: the first two weeks you're there daily — introducing customers and suppliers, handing over passwords, explaining why the Tuesday delivery comes on Wednesday — then it drops to a few hours a week as the buyer takes the wheel.

This block is part of what the buyer bought; in the purchase-price allocation it rides along with goodwill. Which is exactly why you should define it precisely: a fixed number of hours, inside a fixed window, with a log both sides initial. "Reasonable training as needed" is a blank check written against your retirement.

Layer two: the phone window

After the on-site block, agreements typically keep you reachable for 6 to 12 months of phone and email questions — where's the boiler shutoff, what's the deal with the December customer, how do I file the state thing. It costs you little and it protects two things you still care about: your seller note, which gets repaid by a business that keeps running, and your escrow holdback, which releases faster when the buyer isn't drowning.

Layer three: paid consulting — now up to 24 months on SBA deals

Beyond the included hours, you bill. Market rates run $50–$250 an hour depending on the business and how irreplaceable you are; $85 is a common Main Street figure. The news: for SBA loans numbered on or after October 1, 2026, SOP 50 10 8.1 doubled the seller consulting window from 12 to 24 months, extensions included. The unchanged rule sits right beside it: after an SBA-financed sale you cannot remain an officer, director, stockholder, or key employee. Consultant is the only hat the program lets you wear — and now you can wear it twice as long.

Worked example (hypothetical — illustrative rates; your CPA runs the real numbers)

Rosa sells her bakery-café for $420,000 with 150 included training hours. The buyer, nervous, wants her around well past that — roughly 400 extra hours over the next year. At $85/hour, that's $34,000 of consulting income: subject to self-employment tax (~$4,800) plus her 22% federal bracket (~$7,000), she keeps about $22,200.

Alternative: fold the same $34,000 into the purchase price as goodwill. Taxed at 15% long-term capital gains = $5,100, she keeps $28,900 — about $6,700 more for identical hours. The buyer's CPA will push the other way: consulting fees deduct immediately, while goodwill amortizes over 15 years. There's no universally right answer — just a negotiation most sellers don't realize is happening. Have your CPA in the room before the structure is set.

Negotiating your exit without poisoning it

Three clauses do most of the protective work. A fixed hour-block, not a schedule — "150 hours within 90 days" beats "available as the buyer reasonably requires," which can mean every Saturday until someone sues. A signed-off log: hours recorded, initialed weekly, so "you never trained me" can't become a holdback dispute. And in all-cash deals, expect the buyer to want an escrow holdback tied to the training — sensible from their side, fine for you if the release conditions are objective (hours delivered, not satisfaction felt).

What about refusing to stay at all? It's your right, and it's expensive. Buyers and their lenders read "seller gone at closing" as risk — relationships that won't transfer, processes that live in one head. The practical effect is a smaller buyer pool and softer offers. If you genuinely can't stay (health, a move), compensate with documentation: written procedures, a trained second-in-command, recorded walkthroughs. You're replacing your presence with a manual, so start writing it the year before you list — it's a line item in our 12-month prep plan, and the transition terms themselves belong in the LOI, not discovered in the purchase agreement draft.

Last calibration: the transition is also the buyer's final diligence on you. Sellers who teach generously in those first 90 days see their escrow release clean, their seller note perform, and — not incidentally — their name survive intact in a small industry. The deal took five months to close; don't spend the last ninety days of it proving the buyer overpaid.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a seller typically stay after selling?

Two to four weeks full-time, an included training block of roughly 125–150 hours over the first 90 days, then 6–12 months of phone and email. Anything past that is paid consulting, typically $50–$250 an hour.

Can the seller keep working in the business after an SBA sale?

As a consultant only, now for up to 24 months under SOP 50 10 8.1 (loans numbered on or after October 1, 2026). The seller still can't remain an officer, director, stockholder, or key employee.

What if I refuse to stay and train the buyer?

Smaller buyer pool, softer price — lenders and buyers price the risk. If you truly can't stay, substitute documentation and a trained second-in-command, and disclose it early.
