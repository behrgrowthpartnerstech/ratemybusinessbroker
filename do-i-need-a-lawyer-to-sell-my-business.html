Do I Need a Lawyer to Sell My Business If I Have a Broker?

By The Broker Index Team · October 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — hire the lawyer, even with a great broker. They do different jobs. The broker prices the business, markets it quietly, screens buyers, and negotiates terms; the attorney turns the handshake into documents that still protect you two years later. Brokers can't legally do that part: giving legal advice is the unauthorized practice of law, and state regulators like the North Carolina Real Estate Commission spell out that intermediaries may, at most, fill in blanks on pre-printed forms. The buyer will arrive with counsel. The purchase agreement will be drafted by someone's lawyer — the only question is whether any of the lawyers in the room work for you.

What the lawyer does that the broker can't

The purchase agreement is 40-plus pages, and the dangerous ones aren't about price. Representations and warranties define what you're promising about the business; indemnification defines what breaking a promise costs; survival periods define how long the buyer can come back; escrow terms define how much of your money waits in limbo; and the non-compete defines your next five years. A deal attorney negotiates caps, baskets, and sunsets on all of it. They also run the unglamorous mechanics: entity cleanup, consents and contract assignments, lien releases, guarantee releases, and the closing checklist that keeps closing day boring, which is the goal.

One boundary to respect in the other direction: deal lawyers kill deals when they're asked to be businesspeople. You want an attorney who marks the ten clauses that matter and lets the commercial points stay commercial — ask any broker about the deal a $400-an-hour perfectionist negotiated to death. The fix is role clarity: lawyer owns risk language, broker owns price and terms, you own the decision.

What it costs — and what one catch is worth

Worked example (hypothetical)

Priya sells her staffing agency for $700,000. Her deal attorney quotes a flat $12,500 — about 1.8% of the price. Expensive, until the redlines come back.

Catch one: the buyer's draft had uncapped indemnification with a three-year survival period — Priya personally liable, without limit, for any breach. Her attorney negotiates it to a 10% escrow with an 18-month survival: worst case now defined and parked, not open-ended.

Catch two: a quiet double-dip. The draft let the buyer keep $25,000 of accounts receivable that the cash-free, debt-free structure already priced as Priya's. One paragraph, $25,000 — two times her entire legal fee — recovered because someone read the asset schedule against the definitions section. That's the trade in miniature: fees are visible and certain; the clauses are invisible and expensive.

Hiring the right one (and when)

You want a transactional attorney who closes business sales regularly — not your cousin the litigator, not the generalist who did your will. Ask how many purchase agreements they handled in the past year, which side they usually represent, and whether they'll quote flat or capped fees for a defined scope; per Exitwise, small-deal engagements run 1–5% of transaction value, and a defined scope keeps you at the low end. Local bar referral lists and your CPA are better sources than search ads.

On cost control: give the attorney a defined scope and ask what you can prepare yourself — organized contracts, a complete entity file, and clean schedules cut billable hours directly, and the paperwork checklist doubles as that preparation.

Timing: bring them in before you sign the letter of intent, not after. The LOI sets exclusivity, price mechanics, and escrow expectations while you still have leverage; a lawyer who first sees the deal at the purchase-agreement stage is negotiating uphill on terrain the LOI already gave away. An hour of review before signing costs a few hundred dollars and routinely saves the five-figure version of the same conversation later. And loop them in on the broker's listing agreement too — tail periods and fee definitions deserve the same ten minutes of scrutiny.

The clauses that do the quiet damage

A short field guide to where seller money leaks, so you know what you're paying your attorney to hunt. Indemnification caps and baskets: the cap limits your total exposure (10–20% of price is a common ask; uncapped is a hard no), and the basket sets a deductible below which the buyer can't claim — without one, you're litigating $900 disputes. Survival periods: how long each representation can come back to bite; 12–18 months is normal for general reps, longer only for tax and title. Escrow sizing: every dollar in escrow is your money on probation; size and release triggers are negotiable, not standard. Working capital definitions: on deals with a peg, the definition of "working capital" moves real dollars at true-up. Assignment of your receivables: in a cash-free deal, make sure collections flow back to you with a mechanism, not a promise. Guarantee releases: lease, credit line, vendor accounts — each needs an actual release document, not an intention.

Add two situational flags that convert a maybe-lawyer into a definitely-lawyer. If the buyer wants you to carry a note, the security package — personal guarantee, UCC filing, intercreditor terms with the bank — is pure legal craftsmanship, and sloppy drafting there converts directly into uncollectable paper. And if your deal involves partners, family members, trusts, or an estate on either side, the entity work alone justifies the engagement before anyone touches the purchase agreement.

None of these appear in the listing, the marketing, or the handshake. All of them appear in the first draft the buyer's counsel sends, drafted the buyer's way. That asymmetry is the entire case for counsel: the draft is never neutral, and redlines are cheaper than lawsuits by a factor with several zeros.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an attorney if I have a broker?

Yes — different jobs. The broker sells; the attorney drafts and defends the binding documents. Brokers legally can't advise you on the law, and the buyer's side will be represented.

How much does the lawyer cost?

$3K–$8K under $500K; $8K–$25K to $3M; $25K–$75K to $15M. Flat fees of $7,500–$20,000 are common for simple asset sales. Figure one to two percent of a typical Main Street deal.

What do they actually catch?

Uncapped indemnification, oversized escrows, long survival periods, receivables double-dips, overbroad covenants, and missing guarantee releases. One catch commonly pays the whole fee — and the catches cluster in the first draft, which is why the review happens before signing rather than after the dispute.
