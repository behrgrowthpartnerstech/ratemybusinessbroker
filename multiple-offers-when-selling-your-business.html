You Got Multiple Offers for Your Business. How Do You Choose?

By The Broker Index Team · October 21, 2026 · 8 min read

Rank them by expected value, not by headline. An offer is a stack of components — cash at closing, seller notes, earnouts, escrows, employment terms — and each component carries its own odds of actually turning into money. Cash at closing pays at 100%. A seller note pays at whatever your buyer's future looks like. An earnout pays, on market-wide evidence, about one dollar in five. So the choosing method is mechanical: convert every offer to risk-adjusted dollars, then weigh certainty of closing and what life looks like under each buyer. The headline number is marketing. The expected value is the offer.

First, enjoy it — you're in rare air

Multiple simultaneous offers are the payoff of running a real process instead of dating the first inquiry. Upmarket it's now the norm: in the IBBA's Q2 2026 Market Pulse, 87% of $5M+ deals drew three or more offers, and 33% drew ten or more. Main Street sees fewer collisions, which cuts both ways: each offer matters more, and the discipline of comparing them properly matters most. Resist the urge to auction forever, though — offers are perishable, buyers have alternatives, and a seller who shops a signed LOI around town develops a reputation faster than a listing goes stale.

The risk-adjustment, worked

Worked example (hypothetical)

Renata's distribution business draws two offers. Offer A: $900,000 — $650,000 cash, a $150,000 seller note over five years, and a $100,000 earnout tied to revenue targets. Offer B: $830,000 all cash.

Risk-adjust A, component by component. The note: seller-carried paper defaults at a reported 11–14%, and recovery on unsecured notes runs 35–55 cents on the dollar, so Renata's advisor discounts the note by a third → it counts as $100,000. The earnout: market data says roughly 1 in 5 earnout dollars ever pays, but these particular targets look achievable under the buyer's plan, so call it a generous 30 cents → $30,000. Offer A's expected value: $650,000 + $100,000 + $30,000 = $780,000.

Against B's certain $830,000, the "$900K" offer is $50,000 behind. For A to merely tie, its $250,000 of paper would have to deliver $180,000 — 72 cents on the dollar — in a world where paper mostly doesn't. Renata takes B, sleeps nights, and never has to audit anyone's revenue reports.

Beyond the money: certainty, speed, and the next two years

Certainty of closing deserves its own column in your comparison. A financed buyer who hasn't talked to a lender is a maybe wearing a term sheet; one with an SBA pre-qualification letter and proof of funds is a probably. Deals die between LOI and closing at depressing rates — nearly half of broken LOIs fail in diligence and quality-of-earnings review — so an offer from a buyer who's done this before, with financing teed up and reasonable diligence requests, carries value no spreadsheet cell captures. Ask each bidder: financing status, diligence timeline, who's doing their QoE, and what could make them walk.

Your next two years matter too. Offers differ on transition expectations, employment or consulting terms, what happens to your people, and — if you're carrying paper — how entangled you stay. An earnout means you care intensely about a business someone else now runs; read how earnouts actually work before you accept one as "basically price." If you do carry a note, underwrite your buyer like a bank would: credit, courts, liens, references.

Tactically, your broker can go back to finalists with a clean "best and final" — specific asks, one round, no endless ping-pong. The losers deserve a prompt, polite no, because today's runner-up is next quarter's backup if diligence breaks the winner. Then pick, sign the LOI, and stop shopping; exclusivity means it.

Build the comparison grid before you look at names

The clean way to run the decision: a one-page grid, filled in before emotions pick a favorite. Rows: cash at closing; note face value and your risk-adjusted value; earnout maximum and expected value; escrow size and survival period; financing status with evidence; diligence scope and timeline; your required transition commitment; treatment of employees; and any employment or consulting pay. Columns: one per offer. Fill in the dollar rows first and total the risk-adjusted line. Then — only then — look at the softer rows and ask what each buyer costs you in time, entanglement, and sleep. Sellers who build the grid routinely discover that their gut favorite was really "the buyer who flattered me on the walkthrough," which is a fine quality in a dinner guest and an irrelevant one in a counterparty.

The grid also keeps you honest about taxes and timing, two rows sellers skip. Identical headline prices can net differently after the purchase-price allocation each buyer proposes, and a buyer who can close in sixty days is worth real money against one whose lender needs five months — every extra month under exclusivity is a month of business risk you carry for free.

Two grid disciplines worth stating. First, every cell gets evidence or a discount: "SBA pre-approval letter dated this month" is a fact; "financing won't be a problem" is a 20% haircut wearing confidence. Second, the grid is also your negotiation map — if Offer A's buyer is strongest everywhere except the earnout, the counter writes itself: convert half the earnout to closing cash and you have a signable winner. Brokers who run processes well do exactly this, which is one reason competitive sales close at better terms, not just better prices.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always take the highest offer?

Take the highest expected value. Discount notes for default risk, earnouts for the one-in-five payment reality, and weigh financing certainty. Headlines lose to arithmetic.

How common are multiple offers?

At $5M+, very: 87% of deals drew three or more offers in Q2 2026, and a third drew ten or more. On Main Street, simultaneous offers are rarer — which makes running a real marketed process, instead of taking the first call, the whole difference.

How do I compare cash to an earnout?

Multiply the earnout's maximum by honest odds of hitting targets under new management — market-wide, ~20% of earnout dollars pay — then add it to the certain money and compare totals.
