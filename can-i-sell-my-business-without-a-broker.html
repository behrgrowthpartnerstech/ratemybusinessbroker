Can I Sell My Business Without a Broker?

By The Broker Index Team · September 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes. You can sell your business without a broker — no state requires one, plenty of owners do it, and when the buyer is your operations manager or the competitor who's been circling for years, it's often the right call. But "can" and "should" are different questions. A broker on a Main Street deal costs about 10% of the price. Selling alone costs something too; it's just billed in worse currency — mispricing, blown confidentiality, dead months, deals that die in a lender's inbox. Here's the math on both sides.

What the broker route actually costs

Start with the number that makes owners consider going solo. On deals under roughly $1–2 million, the standard arrangement is a success fee of about 10% of the sale price, usually with a minimum fee somewhere between $10,000 and $25,000. Bigger deals shift to tiered formulas like the Double Lehman, where the percentage steps down as the price steps up. We've broken down every variation — retainers, minimums, tail periods — in How Business Brokers Get Paid.

On the market's median deal, that fee is real money: 10% of the Q2 2026 median sale price of $349,250 is about $34,900. Nobody shrugs that off. So the honest question isn't "is the fee big?" It is. The question is whether you can replicate what it buys for less.

What you're signing up to do yourself

Selling a business isn't one job. It's five or six, running in parallel for months, while you also run the business.

You set the price — and pricing is where solo sales go wrong first. Only 14% of owners in BizBuySell's Q2 2026 survey had completed a professional valuation, and unrealistic pricing is the single biggest reason listed businesses fail to sell. You build the story: recast financials, an add-back schedule a stranger's accountant will believe, a summary document buyers take seriously. You find buyers without telling the world — your employees, customers, and suppliers ideally learn about the sale after it closes, which is hard to square with marketing the thing publicly under your own name. You screen the inquiries; most people who answer a business-for-sale ad have neither the money nor the intent to buy. And you package the deal for a bank, because 78% of today's buyers expect to use SBA financing, which means your numbers have to survive SBA 7(a) underwriting, not just a handshake.

All of that takes time even when it's someone's full-time job: the median business took 155 days to sell in Q2 2026, and IBBA Market Pulse data puts listing-to-close at about six months for deals under $500K. Done nights and weekends, it takes longer — and a business whose owner is distracted for a year has a way of shrinking while it waits.

Worked example (hypothetical): the break-even on going it alone

A print shop earns $155,000 in seller's discretionary earnings — right at the market's median cash flow. At the Q2 2026 average multiple of 2.7×, it's worth about $155,000 × 2.7 = $418,500.

Brokered sale at full market value, 10% fee: $418,500 − $41,850 = $376,650 net to the owner (before attorney and closing costs, which both routes pay).

Now the FSBO break-even: with no commission, you keep every dollar — so you match the brokered net by selling at just $376,650, which is exactly 90% of market value. Sell alone above 90% and you come out ahead. Sell below it and the "free" route cost more than the fee.

The catch is that hitting even 90% is not a given. IBBA's Q1 2026 data shows deals under $500K settle at 87% of asking price with professional intermediaries running the process. Going alone, you're betting you can out-negotiate that benchmark, on your first deal, against a buyer who's often done several.

When selling without a broker makes real sense

There are situations where skipping the broker is simply correct, and any honest broker will tell you so.

The big one: you already have the buyer. A key employee, a family member, a competitor who approached you. There's no marketing to do, no confidentiality to manage, no buyer search — the parts of the job a broker's fee mostly pays for. What you need instead is a defensible price and clean paperwork: pay a few thousand dollars for an independent valuation, hire a deal attorney to draft the purchase agreement, and let your CPA structure the tax side. That team might cost $10,000–$20,000 all-in versus $40,000+ in commission.

It also gets more attractive at the very small end. On a $120,000 deal, a $25,000 minimum fee is more than 20% of the price — at that point the commission math stops working, and marketplaces where owners list directly become the practical route.

When the fee earns itself

Flip the situations around and the fee starts looking cheap. No buyer in hand, and the sale needs to stay quiet? Running a confidential process — blind ads, NDAs before financials, screening out the tire-kickers — is the core of the job. Price matters more than speed? A competent broker's whole leverage is having more than one interested buyer at the table; that's how the 98% columns in the chart happen. First sale, financed buyer, complicated add-backs? Someone has to hold the deal together through underwriting, and the Exit Planning Institute's readiness data suggests most owners underestimate that stretch: it's a big part of why 70–80% of listed private businesses never sell at all.

One warning if you do hire help: the fee only earns itself when the broker is good, and the spread between good and bad is enormous. Interview more than one, check their closed-deal history in your size range, and read what past clients say — that's literally why this site exists. A bad broker at 10% is the most expensive option on this page.

The short FSBO playbook

Decided to go alone? Do it like a professional would. Get an independent valuation before you breathe a number to anyone — anchor to real comps, not hope (our guide to reasonable multiples is a starting point). Prepare the business the same way you would for a brokered sale; the 12-month plan applies double when nobody's packaging you. List where buyers actually look — BizBuySell and similar marketplaces let owners list directly for a monthly fee. Use a blind profile, and get an NDA signed before anyone sees financials or your name. Ask every inquiry two questions early: how much liquid cash do you have, and have you spoken to a lender? Silence on either is your answer. And spend properly on the deal attorney; the purchase agreement, escrow, and the closing gauntlet are not DIY territory even when the search was.

FAQ

Is it legal to sell a business without a broker?

Yes. No U.S. state requires a broker to sell a business you own. You'll still want a deal attorney for the purchase agreement and escrow, and a CPA for the tax structure — those two aren't optional just because the broker is.

How much does a business broker charge to sell a business?

On Main Street deals, the standard success fee is about 10% of the sale price, often with a $10,000–$25,000 minimum. Larger deals use tiered formulas like the Double Lehman, where the percentage steps down as the price rises.

When does selling without a broker make sense?

Mostly when you already have the buyer — an employee, family member, or a competitor who approached you — so you're paying for a valuation and a lawyer, not a search. It also makes sense on very small deals where a broker's minimum fee would eat a fifth of the price.
