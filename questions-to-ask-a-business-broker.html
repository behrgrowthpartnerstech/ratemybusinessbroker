The 8 Questions to Ask a Business Broker Before You Sign Anything

By The Broker Index Team · September 30, 2026 · 8 min read

Signing with a business broker means handing a stranger your biggest asset, a 10–15% success fee, and six to twelve months of exclusivity. The questions below are the ones that separate brokers who close deals from brokers who collect listings — and every one of them has a right answer you can check against numbers. Ask all eight before anything gets signed, and write the answers down; you'll want them when the listing agreement arrives.

1. How many businesses like mine have you actually sold?

Not "how long have you been in business." Sold. Your size, your industry, your state, in the last three years. A broker who's closed four restaurants this year knows the buyers, the landlords, and the health-department surprises; a generalist learns on your dime. Only 20–30% of listed businesses ever sell, so you're hiring for closing skill, not listing skill. Ask for addresses and dates, then verify what you can.

2. How did you arrive at that price — and will it survive a lender?

The median small business sells at about 2.7× cash flow ($349,250 median price). A broker proposing something far above that should show comparable sales and the math, not adjectives. Better yet, ask them to run the SBA debt-service numbers on their own price: with most buyers financing through the SBA, a price the coverage ratio rejects isn't a price, it's bait — the classic opener of the overpriced-listing mill. Our SDE guide covers the earnings number everything hangs on.

3. What exactly is the fee — base, minimum, and when is it due?

Three sub-questions hide in here. The rate: 10–15% is market for $100K–$1M deals. The minimum: $10,000–$15,000 flat minimums are common and can turn a small sale into a 20% effective fee. The timing and base: due at closing, on the business price — not due when a "ready, willing and able buyer" appears, and not computed on a base inflated with inventory and real estate. The listing agreement red flags guide shows how much money lives in these clauses.

4. How long is the listing, and what does the tail cover?

Market term is 6–12 months; the tail — their fee rights after expiry — runs anywhere from 6 months to 3 years. The fair answer: a 12-month tail covering only a written list of registered buyers they introduced. "Any buyer, two years" is a claim on a sale they may have nothing to do with.

5. How will you market it without my employees finding out?

The right answer has specifics: a blind listing that describes without identifying, NDAs signed before the name is released, financials shared in stages, and meetings away from the premises. "We'll be discreet" is not a plan. The mechanics are in our confidentiality guide — a broker should rattle them off unprompted.

6. How do you screen buyers before they see my numbers?

Most inquiries are tourists. A disciplined broker demands proof of funds and a buyer profile before the CIM goes out, and pre-qualifies the deal with SBA lenders so financeable buyers move fast. Ask what percentage of inquirers ever get your financials; a good answer is a small one.

7. Who, personally, works my deal?

At multi-agent firms, the rainmaker who pitched you may hand your file to whoever's free. Ask who runs buyer calls, who negotiates, who shows up at closing — by name — and meet that person before signing. Then look that individual up, because reputation varies broker by broker, not firm by firm.

8. Can I talk to a seller whose deal closed — and one whose didn't?

Anyone can produce two happy references. The telling one is the seller whose deal died: did the broker keep working, communicate honestly, and relist intelligently — or vanish? A broker confident enough to hand you a dead-deal reference is telling you something good about themselves.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Two brokers pitch the same $500,000 listing. Broker A: 10% at closing, $15,000 minimum, no upfront fee. Broker B: 12% plus a $5,000 upfront "marketing package," fee due when a ready, willing and able buyer is produced.

If it sells at $450,000: A costs $45,000; B costs $54,000 + $5,000 = $59,000 — a $14,000 swing, about 3% of your proceeds. If it never sells (remember: most listings don't): A costs $0; B kept $5,000. And if a buyer appears at full price but you decline for good reason, B's "ready, willing and able" clause may claim $54,000 for a sale that never happened. Same business — the structure, not the rate, is the money question.

Frequently asked questions

What should I ask a business broker before signing a listing agreement?

Track record with businesses like yours, the valuation math, the complete fee structure, term and tail, the confidential marketing plan, buyer screening, who personally works the deal, and references from closed and dead deals.

How much do business brokers charge?

Typically 10–15% on $100K–$1M deals, with $10,000–$15,000 minimums on small ones. The structure — when it's due and on what base — moves more money than the headline rate.

What is a tail period in a broker agreement?

The post-expiry window where the broker still earns a fee if you sell to a buyer they introduced. Push for 12 months or less, limited to a written list of registered buyers.
