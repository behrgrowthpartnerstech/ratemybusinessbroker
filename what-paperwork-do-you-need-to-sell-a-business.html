What Paperwork Do You Need to Sell a Business? The Full Checklist

By The Broker Index Team · November 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Here's the checklist in one breath. Pile one, financials: three years of business tax returns, a year-to-date P&L, a balance sheet, and a documented add-back schedule. Pile two, the operating file: the lease, your top-20 customer and top-20 vendor contracts, licenses and permits, an equipment list with titles and UCC liens, an employee roster including the handshake deals, and corporate records. Pile three, the documents the sale itself creates: NDA, CIM, LOI, then the asset purchase agreement with its bill of sale, assignments, non-compete, and IRS Form 8594. Gather pile one before you list — roughly half of collapsed deals die in exactly this stretch of paper.

Pile one: the financials

Tax returns anchor everything because they're the one document with no incentive to flatter — nobody inflates income to the IRS. The add-back schedule is the bridge from the returns to SDE, the number the price is built on: the Q3 2026 market runs a $350,000 median at 2.30×, closing at 93.6% of asking, per the BizBuySell Insight Report. Every add-back needs an invoice, a statement, or a ledger line behind it. An add-back without paper is a story, and lenders don't finance stories.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Sylvia's landscaping company claims $160,000 SDE, including $14,000 of add-backs with no paper behind them — a roof repair paid to a cash-only roofer with no invoice, and truck miles never logged. Diligence strikes them; documented SDE is $146,000.

At 2.3×: $160,000 × 2.3 = $368,000 versus $146,000 × 2.3 = $335,800. The missing receipts cost $32,200 — about $2,300 for every undocumented thousand. The roofer saved her a few hundred dollars. The filing cabinet would have been the better investment.

Pile two: the operating file

A typical small business runs on 20–50 contracts, and a buyer will want to read the ones that matter: the lease first — assignment runs 60–90 days, so it goes to the top of the pile — then the top-20 customer and vendor contracts, flagged for anti-assignment language (the full consent problem is covered in do contracts transfer). Pull a UCC lien search on your own business before the buyer does; discovering an old equipment lien in closing week is a fire drill you can schedule out of existence today. And write down the unwritten: the handshake bonus, the informal Friday schedule, the verbal supplier discount. Each one becomes a diligence finding if the buyer learns it from someone other than you.

The employee roster deserves a special mention because it's where honest owners accidentally hide things. The roster a buyer wants isn't names and titles — it's compensation, tenure, any employment agreements, and every informal arrangement that walks like a liability: the verbal severance promise, the unofficial four-day week, the bonus that's "always been" paid in December. Same discipline for licenses and permits: list each one, who holds it (you personally or the entity — it matters), and whether it transfers. A liquor license that takes a fresh 90-day application is a closing-date fact the buyer should learn from your file, not from the state's website in week eleven.

Pile three: the deal paper

These documents don't exist yet — the sale creates them, in order: the NDA, the CIM, the LOI, and finally the purchase agreement with its bill of sale, contract assignments, and your non-compete. One form sellers forget: IRS Form 8594, where buyer and seller file matching allocations of the price — and where the tax bill gets shaped. SBA-financed deals add a layer: an independent valuation on every change of ownership, and at $3M+ financed, a mandatory quality of earnings review. Spoiler: your add-back schedule, again.

Why the paperwork decides the closing

Axial's dead-deal data puts 25.3% of broken LOIs in diligence and another 21.3% in quality-of-earnings — 46.6% combined — across an average 106 days under exclusivity. Due diligence is an open-book test of your filing system, pass-fail, with your sale price attached. A clean file doesn't just survive; it closes faster, renegotiates less, and leaves less money parked in escrow holdbacks. Messy files don't usually kill deals outright. They bleed them, a price cut at a time.

It also pays to understand why buyers weight the piles the way they do. The financials answer "is the income real?" The operating file answers "does the income survive the handover?" — a business whose lease expires in eight months or whose biggest contract dies on assignment has real income with no future. And the deal paper allocates risk for whatever the first two piles missed, through representations, indemnities, and escrows. Sellers who grasp that hierarchy stop treating document requests as bureaucratic hazing and start seeing them as the buyer slowly agreeing to wire money. The faster the pile answers, the faster the wire.

When to start: 90–180 days before listing

CT Acquisitions pegs sell-side document prep at 90–180 days, and that squares with what the pile actually contains: a year-end closing from your CPA, a lien search, a contract read-through, and a documented add-back schedule don't happen in a weekend. Our 12-month preparation guide schedules the whole runway. One more use for the checklist: when you interview brokers, ask for their day-one document request list. A broker whose list looks like this page is running a real process. A broker with no list is planning to learn your business from the buyer's questions — at your expense.

A practical way to run the project: open three literal folders — paper or digital, buyers don't care — and give yourself one month per pile. Month one, the financials, with your CPA closing out the add-back schedule. Month two, the operating file, with the attorney reading the lease and the top contracts. Month three, the gaps the first two months exposed: the missing equipment title, the handshake deal that needs writing down, the permit renewal that was due anyway. By the time a broker asks for documents, you hand over a file instead of a promise — and in this market, a seller with a file is already ahead of most of the inventory.

Frequently asked questions

What documents do I need to sell my business?

Three piles: financials (3 years of returns, YTD P&L, balance sheet, documented add-backs), the operating file (lease, top-20 contracts, licenses, equipment and liens, employee roster, corporate records), and the deal paper the sale creates (NDA, CIM, LOI, APA, assignments, non-compete, Form 8594).

Do I need audited financials?

No — returns plus internal statements carry Main Street deals. Independent review arrives via the SBA's required valuation, and a mandatory QoE on $3M+ financed deals.

How many years of records?

Three years of returns, sometimes five — with the trailing twelve months getting the hardest look, so the YTD P&L has to reconcile with the returns behind it.
