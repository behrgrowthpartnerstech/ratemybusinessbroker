How Long Does It Take to Sell a Business? Real 2026 Timelines

By The Broker Index Team · September 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Plan on six to eleven months. In the latest IBBA Market Pulse survey data (Q1 2026), the median time from listing to closing was 6 months for businesses under $500K, 7 months for $500K–$1M, and about 9 months for everything from $1M to $50M. And that clock only starts at listing: BizBuySell's Q2 2026 Insight Report shows service businesses spending a median 155 days on the market before a buyer is even found — manufacturing took 247. Anyone who promises to sell your business in 90 days is describing the exception, not the plan.

Below is where those months actually go, stage by stage — and, more usefully, which of them you can shorten and which ones quietly double when a business is overpriced or underprepared.

The five stages, and what each one really takes

StageTypical durationWhat's happening
1. Preparation & valuation2–8 weeksRecasting financials, calculating SDE and add-backs, setting the asking price, assembling the CIM.
2. Confidential marketing3–6 monthsThe listing goes live (blind ads, buyer databases); buyers sign NDAs, review the CIM, and tour the business.
3. Offers & LOI2–6 weeksNegotiating price, terms, seller financing, and transition — ending in a signed letter of intent.
4. Due diligence & financing2–4 monthsThe buyer verifies everything; the lender underwrites. IBBA pegs LOI-to-close at 2 months under $500K, 4 months above $1M.
5. Closing & transition2–4 weeksPurchase agreement, lease assignment, licenses, escrow — then keys and training period.

Notice where the calendar actually lives: stages 2 and 4. Everything else is measured in weeks. The two questions that decide your timeline are how fast a qualified buyer appears and whether that buyer's financing survives underwriting.

Bigger deals take longer — but not much longer

What quietly adds months

Overpricing — the #1 timeline killer. An overpriced business doesn't just sit; it goes stale, and buyers read months-on-market as a defect signal. Worse, even if an optimistic buyer bites, the price still has to clear the buyer's lender. That's where deals die at month seven instead of month two. Here's the mechanism:

Hypothetical: why overpricing fails in underwriting, not on the market

A service business earns $200,000 SDE. At a market-typical 2.8× multiple it's priced at $560,000. A buyer puts 10% down and borrows $504,000 via SBA 7(a) at 10.5% over 10 years — about $6,801/month, or $81,609/year in debt service. Cash available after a $75,000 replacement salary for the new owner is $125,000, so DSCR = $125,000 ÷ $81,609 = 1.53. Lenders like to see ~1.25 (the SBA floor is 1.15) — this deal sails through.

Price the same business at 3.5× ($700,000) and the loan grows to $630,000 — $8,501/month, $102,011/year. Now DSCR = $125,000 ÷ $102,011 = 1.23 — below the 1.25 comfort line. The lender asks for a bigger down payment, a price cut, or a seller note… four to six months after you listed. Illustrative example, not a quote.

Financing friction generally. BizBuySell's Q2 2026 report found nearly 78% of buyers expect to use SBA financing, while brokers cite tighter credit conditions as a top source of deal friction. SBA underwriting, third-party valuation, and life-insurance requirements routinely consume 60–90 days after the LOI — read the SBA's 7(a) loan overview to see how many boxes a buyer must tick.

Messy books. Every add-back you can't document is a week of due-diligence argument. Cash revenue that never hit the P&L can't be sold, and commingled personal expenses invite the buyer's accountant to re-open the price.

Industry. BizBuySell's full-year 2025 data shows median days on market ranging from 151 days for route businesses to 412 days for laundromats — the buyer pool for your specific kind of business matters more than your asking price's second digit.

What actually speeds a sale up

Price to the market, not to your retirement number. In the Q1 2026 Market Pulse, businesses under $500K sold at a median 87% of asking price, while the $500K–$1M bracket achieved 100% — largely because those sellers priced realistically to begin with. A price a lender can underwrite on day one saves you the month-seven renegotiation.

Get SBA-prequalified before listing. A broker who has a lender pre-screen the deal removes the single biggest source of delay. It also flushes out DSCR problems while you can still fix the price quietly.

Consider seller financing. There's a structural gap in today's market: 90% of buyers expect seller financing, but only 29% of owners plan to offer it (BizBuySell, Q2 2026). A 10–20% seller note widens your buyer pool, signals confidence, and can shave months off the search — it's also a lever brokers use to defend price. Understand the trade-offs before you offer it.

Prepare the diligence folder before the buyer asks. Three years of tax returns, interim P&Ls, lease, licenses, equipment list, payroll summary. A seller who answers document requests in days instead of weeks compresses stage 4 dramatically.

Pick the right intermediary. A broker who actively markets, pre-qualifies buyers, and manages the lender timeline is the difference between six months and sixteen — and between closing and quietly expiring. The timeline nobody counts: before you list

Everything above measures listing to closing. The sellers who hit the short end of every range typically spent 6–12 months before listing: cleaning up financials, documenting processes, reducing owner dependence, and locking in the lease. If your horizon allows it, that preparation year buys back more calendar than anything a broker can do after the listing goes live — and usually adds to the price too.

FAQ

How long does it take to sell a small business?

Six to eleven months in most cases. IBBA Market Pulse data for Q1 2026 puts the median at 6 months from listing to close under $500K and about 9 months above $1M, and BizBuySell's Q2 2026 report shows service businesses spending a median 155 days just finding a buyer.

Why do business sales take so long?

A sale is four sequential projects: packaging the business, finding and qualifying a buyer, surviving due diligence, and closing the buyer's financing. Financing is the slowest link — nearly 78% of buyers expect SBA loans, and underwriting routinely adds 60–90 days after the LOI.

Can I sell my business in 3 months?

It happens, but it's the exception — usually a pre-identified buyer, clean books, realistic pricing, and cash or seller financing instead of a bank loan. Treat any broker's 90-day promise as a marketing claim to probe, not a plan.
