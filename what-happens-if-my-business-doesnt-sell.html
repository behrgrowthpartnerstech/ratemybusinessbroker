What Happens If Your Business Doesn't Sell?

By The Broker Index Team · October 23, 2026 · 9 min read

If your business doesn't sell, you're in the majority: by common industry estimates, only 20–30% of listed businesses ever close. What happens next is mostly up to you — the listing expires, your broker agreement ends (watch the tail clause; more on that below), and you choose between five options: reprice meaningfully, fix what made the deal unfinanceable, restructure the offer with seller financing or an earnout, pull the listing and spend a year preparing properly, or wind the business down. The worst move is the common one: relisting at the same price with a new broker and hoping different marketing fixes the same math.

First, diagnose: why didn't it sell?

Deals die for boring, repeatable reasons. Axial's dead-deal data on broken letters of intent shows 46.6% collapse in diligence and quality-of-earnings review — the books didn't support the story — after an average of 106 days under exclusivity. Listings that never even reach an LOI usually fail earlier, on price. And price failures are rarely about buyer psychology. They're about a lender's calculator.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Sam's machine shop produces $140,000 of SDE. His broker listed it at 4.0× — $560,000 — because Sam needed that number for retirement. A buyer putting 10% down borrows $504,000; at roughly 10% over 10 years that's about $79,925 a year in loan payments. The buyer's cash available after paying themselves a $70,000 salary is $70,000. Coverage: 70,000 ÷ 79,925 = 0.88. Lenders want about 1.25. No bank in America writes that loan, so no financeable buyer ever appeared. The listing didn't fail. It never existed, financially speaking.

Repriced at 2.5× — $350,000 — the loan drops to $315,000 and the payment to about $49,953 a year. Coverage: 70,000 ÷ 49,953 = 1.40. Financeable on day one. Same shop, same earnings. The only thing that changed was arithmetic.

Lender coverage benchmarks: ~1.25 typical, 1.15 SBA floor. See SBA 7(a) terms.

The five options, honestly ranked

Reprice — properly. BizBuySell's guidance from listing data is blunt: a 10–12% adjustment renews interest; a 3% shave convinces nobody. Run the lender math above at the new number before you publish it.

Fix the financeability. Sometimes the price is fine and the file is the problem: undocumented add-backs, returns that don't match the P&L, a lease with 18 months left. Those are fixable in a quarter. The IBBA's Market Pulse shows deals that do close are increasingly competitive — 87% of larger deals drew three or more offers — so buyers exist. They just skip unfinanceable files.

Restructure. A seller note for 15–30% of the price, or an earnout tied to revenue, can bridge a valuation gap a price cut can't. You're taking buyer credit risk to get your number, so read up on seller financing and earnouts before offering either.

Pull it and re-prep. Running the business well for a year beats marketing it badly for another year. Clean the add-backs, document everything, grow the trailing twelve months, and come back. Our 12-month preparation plan is the checklist. A business that didn't sell at $560K after drifting isn't closer to selling; one that grew into a defensible $420K ask is.

Wind down. If earnings are fading and you're done, an orderly liquidation beats a slow bleed. It usually recovers far less than a going-concern sale — which is exactly why it's last on this list, and why even money-losing businesses are often worth selling rather than closing.

The clock is part of the diagnosis

Before declaring the listing dead, compare your timeline to the market's. The median business that does sell spends 155 days on the market — five months — and that's the median, meaning half take longer. A listing that's quiet at day 90 isn't failing; it's normal. A listing with zero inquiries at day 90 is a different animal: that's a price or packaging problem, because even overpriced businesses usually draw curiosity. And a listing with lots of inquiries but no offers is a third animal — buyers are looking, doing their own lender math, and walking. That pattern is the clearest possible signal that the ask doesn't survive the coverage calculation, and it's fixable the same week you accept it.

Ask your broker for the funnel numbers: how many views, how many NDAs signed, how many CIMs sent, how many meetings, how many offers. A broker who can't produce those numbers isn't running a process — they posted an ad. A broker who can produce them has handed you the diagnosis for free, because each stage of the funnel fails for a different reason. Views without NDAs means a weak teaser. NDAs without offers means the book doesn't match the ad. Offers that die in diligence means the books don't match the book.

The tail-clause trap when you change brokers

Before you relist with someone new, reread the expired agreement. Nearly every listing contract has a tail (protection period) entitling the old broker to a full commission if you sell to a buyer they introduced — commonly 6–24 months. Get the old broker's written list of introduced prospects, and make the new agreement carve those buyers out or handle them at a reduced fee. Skip this step and you can owe two full commissions on one closing. We walk through the mechanics, with the dollar math, in how to fire your business broker, and the clause belongs on your red-flag checklist before you sign anything new.

While you're at it, be honest about whether the broker was the problem. Did they ever show you the lender math? Did you get buyer feedback, or silence? A broker who priced to win the listing rather than to close it cost you a year. Check reviews before you hire the next one.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of businesses actually sell?

By common industry estimates, 20–30% of listings close. The rest expire, get pulled, or wind down — most often because the asking price couldn't survive a lender's coverage math.

Should I lower the price if my business isn't selling?

Cut meaningfully or don't bother: 10–12% renews interest, per BizBuySell. And run the financing math first — if a buyer's loan payment at your price eats more than ~80% of cash flow, price is the disease and marketing is a placebo.

What is a tail clause in a listing agreement?

A clause that pays your expired broker a full commission if you later sell to anyone they introduced, typically for 6–24 months. Get the introduced-buyer list in writing before relisting with a new broker.
