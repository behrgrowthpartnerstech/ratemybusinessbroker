Can a Real Estate Agent Sell My Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Legally? In most states, yes — and in about 17 states a real estate license is actually required to broker business sales. So your cousin with the realtor's license may be perfectly legal. The better question is whether someone whose trade is houses can run a sale where the asset is cash flow, the marketing is secret, and the buyer's bank underwrites a loan against earnings. Those are different jobs that happen to share the word "sale."

The licensing map, quickly

Per Business Brokerage Press's state-licensing survey, roughly 17 states require a real estate license to broker business sales: AK, AZ, CA, CO, FL, GA, ID, MN, NE, NV, OR, SD, UT, WA, WI, and WY. Nevada stacks a separate business-broker permit on top of the RE license. Illinois skips the license and uses registration under its Business Brokers Act instead. Most other states require nothing specific at all. The practical upshot: in license states, good business brokers hold the real estate license as a formality. The license is paperwork. The toolkit is the hire.

Why it's a different trade

Four places the toolkits diverge. Pricing: an agent prices off comps; a business is priced off SDE times a multiple — the Q3 2026 market median is a $350,000 sale at 2.30× for sub-$1M deals, closing at 93.6% of asking, per the BizBuySell Insight Report. How that recast works is its own discipline (see how brokers value a business). Marketing: houses win on maximum exposure — the MLS, the yard sign, the open house. Businesses sell through blind listings, because exposure is exactly what kills them. Time and odds: Redfin's data puts the median house at 49 days on market; businesses run a 155-day median, and DueDilio estimates only 20–30% of listings ever sell at all. The deal itself: asset-vs-stock structure, SBA coverage ratios, lease assignment — plus fees of 10–12% with $10–25K minimums, versus the 5–6% total on a home sale. You're not paying more for the same service. It's a different service.

Sources: Redfin (June 2026 update); BizBuySell Insight Report.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Carla owns a commercial cleaning company: revenue $520,000, SDE $110,000. Her cousin — a residential agent — lists it the way you'd price a house with a gut feel: 1× revenue, $520,000. A buyer tries to finance 90%: a $468,000 loan at 10% over 10 years is $6,185/month = $74,220/year. Cash available is $110,000 minus a $55,000 manager salary = $55,000. Coverage: 0.74. No bank writes that loan. The listing sits for a year, going stale in a market where buyers can see the listing date.

Priced like a business — $110,000 × 2.3 = $253,000 — the same buyer's 90% loan is $227,700, costing $36,109/year. Coverage: 1.52. Financeable on day one. The "aggressive" listing didn't get Carla more money; it got her zero offers and a shopworn business.

The fee gap deserves its own paragraph, because it's where sellers talk themselves into the cheaper option. A home sale costs 5–6% total, split between two agents. A business sale costs 10–12% with $10,000–$25,000 minimums, one firm. That looks like double the price until you itemize what the business fee buys: the SDE recast, the lender-tested pricing, the blind marketing apparatus, buyer screening against 10–30 NDAs, and 100-plus days of diligence management. An agent charging 6% to post your business on the MLS with a photo of the storefront isn't half price. It's full price for the wrong product — and the confidentiality cost alone, once employees and competitors see the listing, can exceed the entire fee difference.

When the agent is the right call

Fairness requires the flip side. When the asset mostly is real estate — self-storage, a motel, a car wash — commercial agents with genuine business-opportunity track records can be exactly right. Same when the building sells with the business and the real estate is the bigger number. The test isn't the license on the wall; it's the deal sheet. An agent who's closed six operating businesses is a business broker with a different business card.

There's also a hybrid worth knowing: in the 17 license states, many of the best business brokers are technically real estate licensees, because the law requires it. Don't let the license fool you in either direction. A CBI designation, a wall of closed business transactions, and fluency in SDE math matter; the card stock doesn't. The inverse trap is also real — a few residential agents collect business listings as a sideline, let them sit unsold for a year at hope-based prices, and quietly poison the local market's perception of the business along the way. Businesses, unlike houses, get shopworn: buyers can see the listing date, and 300 days on market reads as "something's wrong," even when the only thing wrong was the first price.

The interview test

Whoever you're considering — agent, broker, or hybrid — four questions sort them fast. How many operating businesses (not buildings) have you sold in the last three years? Walk me through how you'd price mine — if the answer doesn't include the letters S-D-E, keep interviewing. How will you market it without naming it? And which SBA lenders do you work with? Our full list of questions to ask a business broker goes deeper, and how brokers get paid explains what the fee buys: 10% of a $350,000 median sale is $35,000, which is real money — and it should buy a specialist, not a generalist with a lockbox key.

Frequently asked questions

Can a realtor legally sell a business?

In most states, yes — and about 17 states require the real estate license for business brokerage. Nevada adds a separate permit; Illinois uses registration; most states require nothing. Legal isn't the bar, though. Qualified is.

Do you need a license to be a business broker?

Depends on the state — roughly 17 require an RE license, Illinois requires registration, most require nothing specific. In license states, good business brokers carry the RE license as a formality.

Real estate agent or business broker?

Asset-heavy deal where the building leads: a commercial agent with real business-opportunity experience can win. Operating business: hire whoever prices off SDE, markets blind, and knows SBA lending — whatever their card says. Time matters too; see how long a business sale takes.
