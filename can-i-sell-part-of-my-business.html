Can I Sell Just Part of My Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 12, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes — partial sales come in three flavors: sell a minority stake and keep control, sell a majority and keep a slice (rollover equity), or sell one location or asset outright. Two catches before you price anything. Partial stakes sell at discounts of 15–40% off their pro-rata share, and the discounts stack. And the institutional market for partial stakes starts around $1.5 million of earnings — far above the $350,000 median Main Street sale — so at typical small-business size, your realistic buyer is probably already inside the building.

Why 30% of $800K isn't $240K

Appraisers apply two discounts to minority stakes, per CT Acquisitions: a discount for lack of control (DLOC, 10–30% — the holder can't set salaries, distributions, or the exit) and a discount for lack of marketability (DLOM, median 22%, range 15–40% — there's no liquid market to resell into). They compound multiplicatively: 20% then 22% is 1 − (0.80 × 0.78) = 37.6% off, not 42%. Badly papered stakes can reach 40–60% total; strong protections — a put right, veto rights, guaranteed distributions — compress the stack toward 25%. A minority stake in a private company is a seat in someone else's car. The market prices the seat, not the car.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Marisol's sign-fabrication shop earns $320,000 SDE; at 2.5× the whole business is worth $800,000. She wants to sell 30%. Pro-rata: $240,000. An outside passive investor applies the full stack: $240,000 × 0.80 × 0.78 ≈ $150,000 — 37.6% off. Her production manager, who already works there and trusts the books, negotiates a single −15%: $204,000.

The manager's SBA math works, too: 10% down is $20,400; the $183,600 loan at 10%/10yr costs $2,426/month = $29,115/year. Distributable profit is $320,000 minus Marisol's $110,000 market salary = $210,000; his 30% share is $63,000 — coverage of about 2.2 against the 1.25 benchmark.

Path one: sell to someone already inside

At Main Street size, the natural partial buyer is an employee, a family member, or an existing partner — people who don't price in the information risk a stranger would (see selling to an employee or family member and partner buyouts). SBA rules under SOP 50 10 8.1 treat partial changes of ownership as owner buyouts, per Starfield & Smith: at least one original owner stays in the business and personally guarantees the loan regardless of retained percentage; an outside individual not currently employed there can acquire less than 50% but can't become the largest shareholder; coverage of 1.25 is computed on historicals — projections don't count — and the 10% injection applies.

Path two: sell most, keep a slice

Rollover equity flips the ratio: the buyer takes 75–90%, you keep 10–25%, per BizBuySell's rollover guide — the celebrated "second bite of the apple" if the buyer grows it and sells again. Honest caveats: PE preferred stock often accrues 8–10% a year ahead of you, and in a flat outcome that waterfall can eat 30–50% of your rollover's value. And since June 2025, SBA rules say a seller keeping any stake — even 5% — personally guarantees the entire loan for at least two years, as co-borrower, in a stock purchase. Keeping 10% is not a souvenir. It's a co-signature.

The third flavor — selling one location or a divisible asset — escapes the discount math entirely, because a whole location sold outright is a control sale, priced like any other business. The work there is separation: carving out the location's own P&L, allocating shared costs honestly, and untangling whatever the locations share — staff, inventory systems, the one delivery van. If your second location has never had its own books, building them is the first year of the project, not the last week.

When the institutional door opens

Minority-stake funds and growth equity live upstairs: the institutional floor sits around $1.5M+ of EBITDA (SBICs reach down to ~$750K–$1M), growth equity writes $2–10M checks into $5–50M businesses, and majority recaps run a median 22% rollover (range 10–40%) through a 3–6 month process — the territory of an M&A advisor, not a Main Street broker. Below that floor there's no auction for your slice. The price gets set at the kitchen table, not an auction.

One more structural note on rollover deals: the quality of your 10–25% depends almost entirely on paper you negotiate at closing, not on the buyer's spreadsheet. Tag-along rights so you exit when they do, information rights so you see monthly numbers, and a clear answer on whether your shares sit beside the buyer's or beneath their preferred stack. Sellers routinely spend weeks negotiating the headline price and an afternoon on the rollover terms, which is exactly backwards for the piece of the deal they'll live with longest.

Before you sell a slice, ask why

Good reasons: de-risking your net worth, buying out a co-owner, bonding a key employee to the business, staging your exit. A bad one: raising working capital — at a 37% discount, equity is spectacularly expensive money next to a loan. And whoever buys in arrives with rights: veto power over big decisions, monthly financials, and by CT Acquisitions' estimate 10–20% more of your time spent on investor relations. Price the slice off a real valuation built on documented SDE, and if what you really want is cash out plus a gradual handoff, compare it against seller financing a full exit — the note often nets more cash, sooner, with no co-owner across the table for the next decade. General information, not legal, tax, or investment advice — partial-sale structures need an attorney and CPA at the table.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell 50% and keep running it?

Yes — expect below-pro-rata pricing, real investor rights, and, with SBA money involved, a continuing role plus a personal guarantee.

How much is 25% of my business worth?

On a $1M business, typically $140,000–$190,000 after the discounts stack — not $250,000. Protections for the buyer compress the discount; their absence widens it.

Can you get an SBA loan for part of a business?

Yes, under the owner-buyout rules: an original owner stays and guarantees, an outside buyer stays under 50% and can't be largest shareholder, 1.25 coverage on historicals, 10% injection. Details in our SBA guide.
