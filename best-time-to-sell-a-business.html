When Is the Best Time to Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

The best time to sell is two to three years into a rise you can document, while you still have energy left to run a five-month process. That's the whole answer. It comes from watching three clocks — the market's, the business's, and yours — and noticing that the one everyone asks about, the calendar, matters least of all.

Clock one: the market

The current readout is decent. The Q3 2026 Insight Report shows 2,132 closed transactions, a median sale price of $350,000 — flat on the quarter but up 17% year over year — median cash flow of $156,000 (up 5%), sub-$1M deals at a 2.30× average multiple, and sellers collecting 93.6% of asking. Sentiment is balanced: buyer confidence 49, seller confidence 47, and 27% of owners say they're considering selling sooner than planned. Interest rates move this clock more than anything — CT Acquisitions pegs a 100-basis-point rate move at roughly 0.3–0.7× of multiple, because most buyers borrow (the mechanics live in our SBA guide). But you can't schedule your exit around the Fed. Watch this clock; don't worship it.

What the market clock is genuinely good for is calibration, not timing. If the median business is selling at 93.6% of asking in your size band, a broker promising 120% of the comparable math is selling you a listing agreement, not a likely outcome. If 27% of owners are considering selling sooner, your buyer pool is sharing you with more inventory next year than this year. Read the quarterly report the way a farmer reads a forecast: it won't tell you when to plant, but it keeps you from pricing the harvest in a drought.

Clock two: the business — the one that pays

Buyers pay for the way up. CT Acquisitions reports from its own deal experience that 15%+ growth adds half a turn to a turn and a half of multiple, while a 5% decline subtracts the same or forces earnout-heavy structures — trajectory alone can swing price 30–50% on identical earnings. And the sobering one: roughly 60% of owners who delay a planned sale by 12+ months underperform their original window. Growth stories also have to survive due diligence, which is where undocumented ones go to die — and a declining business is a different sale entirely (see selling a loss-maker).

Worked example (hypothetical)

Tomas runs a plumbing company at $180,000 SDE. Today, at 2.6×, it's worth $468,000. He's tempted to wait a year. If the year goes well — up 10% — he sells $198,000 × 2.6 = $514,800, gaining $46,800. If it goes badly — down 15%, and the multiple compresses to 2.2× — he sells $153,000 × 2.2 = $336,600, losing $131,400.

Treat it as a coin flip and the expected value of waiting is 0.5 × $46,800 − 0.5 × $131,400 ≈ −$42,300. The upside is a raise; the downside is a cliff. That asymmetry is why brokers keep saying "sell on the way up" — it's not a slogan, it's the shape of the payoff curve.

Clock three: you

Burnout lands in the books before it lands in your calendar — deferred maintenance, flat marketing, the key hire you never made. By the time you feel done, the numbers have usually been saying it for a year, and you're selling clock two's downside case. The exit-planning world's much-quoted figure (from the Exit Planning Institute) is that around 75% of owners regret the sale within a year — mostly the ones who sold exhausted, without a plan for the day after. Selling takes five months of process (here's the timeline) plus a transition period after. Budget energy for both.

The personal clock has one more hand on it: what you're selling into. Owners with a concrete plan for the day after — a next venture, a role in the community, even a disciplined year of nothing — negotiate better, because they aren't secretly hoping the deal falls through. Owners with no plan drag their feet in diligence, flinch at the finish line, and sometimes torch good deals over small terms. Buyers can smell both. If you can't describe your first Monday after closing, that's a signal the personal clock hasn't actually struck, whatever the fatigue says.

The calendar, since you asked

Viking Mergers' guidance is qualitative and honest: buyer activity picks up early in the year and again after Labor Day, and there's no bad time for a prepared business. With a 155-day median listing period, whatever month you start, you'll finish in a different season anyway. The one date with real money on it: a December versus January closing is a tax-year decision — push the gain into the year where it's taxed best, a call to make with your CPA (our tax guide has the framework).

Seasonality does matter in one narrow case: genuinely seasonal businesses. A landscaping company shows its best trailing-twelve-months just after peak season; a ski shop, the opposite. Listing so that diligence lands on strong months is a real, if modest, edge — and it's about which numbers the buyer's accountant averages, not about when buyers feel like shopping. Your broker should be able to articulate that timing in the first meeting without being asked.

The 2–3 year runway

Whichever clock says go, the preparation window is fixed: lenders want 2–3 years of returns that match the statements, per BizBuySell's guidance — so the best time to start preparing was two years ago, and the second best is this quarter. Clean up the SDE recast, build the document file, and run the 12-month checklist. A prepared business gets to choose its moment. An unprepared one takes whatever moment it gets.

Frequently asked questions

Is 2026 a good time to sell?

The market's solid — $350K median (+17% YoY), 2.30×, 93.6% of asking. But your trajectory outweighs the market: documented growth sells well in nearly any year.

What's the best month to sell?

None worth planning around. Activity rises early-year and post-Labor Day, but a 155-day listing straddles seasons regardless. December-vs-January closing is the real calendar call, and it's a tax decision.

Should I wait for more growth?

Growth earns a premium, but the bet is asymmetric: the downside year costs far more than the upside year gains, and ~60% of owners who delay underperform. Sell into strength.
