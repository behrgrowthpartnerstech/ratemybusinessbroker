Should I Offer Seller Financing When I Sell My Business?

By The Broker Index Team · September 20, 2026 · 9 min read

In most cases, yes — offer some. Seller financing means you accept part of the purchase price as a promissory note the buyer pays off over a few years, and it's how the majority of small businesses actually change hands: roughly 80% of small business sales include it in some form, and deals that offer it tend to fetch 20% to 30% more than all-cash deals. The catch is dosage. Carrying 10% to 20% of the price with real security behind it is a smart trade. Carrying 60% for a buyer with nothing down is a hobby called "collections."

There's also a gap you should know about before your first buyer meeting. In BizBuySell's Q2 2026 Insight Report, 90% of buyers said they expect seller financing to be available. Only 29% of owners plan to offer it, and almost half say flatly that they won't. Whichever side of that gap you stand on decides how big your buyer pool is.

What seller financing actually is

Strip away the jargon and it's an IOU with paperwork. At closing, the buyer pays part of the price in cash (theirs, a bank's, or both) and signs a promissory note for the rest. The note states the amount, the interest rate, the term, the monthly payment, and what happens if payments stop. You become the buyer's second lender, usually behind the bank.

Buyers love it for an obvious reason: it stretches their cash. But there's a second reason that works in your favor. A seller who carries a note is telling every buyer, in a way no marketing paragraph can, that they believe the business will keep producing after they hand over the keys. Buyers read a flat "no financing, cash only" the opposite way, fairly or not.

What normal terms look like in 2026

For Main Street deals, the ranges have been stable for years. The note usually covers 10% to 30% of the purchase price, with 10% to 20% most common when a bank loan is also in the deal. Interest has typically run 6% to 8% — priced off the risk you're taking on this specific buyer, not off whatever the ten-year Treasury did this week. Terms run three to seven years, with five the workhorse.

Two rules of thumb keep a note sane. First, the buyer's total annual debt payments should leave the business comfortable: lenders want a debt-service coverage ratio around 1.25, meaning $1.25 of available cash flow for every $1 of loan payments (the SBA's floor is 1.15). Second, your note's payment alone shouldn't eat more than about a third of annual cash flow. If the numbers only work when everything goes perfectly, they don't work.

The math on a $432,000 deal (hypothetical)

Say your shop earns $160,000 in seller's discretionary earnings and prices at the market's 2.7× median multiple: $432,000. A typical structure: the buyer puts down 30% ($129,600), a bank lends 50% ($216,000 over 10 years), and you carry 20% ($86,400 at 8% over 5 years).

Your note pays you $1,752 a month — about $21,000 a year — and by the end you've collected $105,113 on that $86,400 slice. That's $18,713 of interest you'd never see in an all-cash deal.

Now the safety check. Total debt service is your $21,023 plus roughly $34,975 to the bank: $55,998 a year. Give the new owner a $70,000 salary and the business still has $90,000 available, so coverage is $90,000 ÷ $55,998 = 1.61 — comfortably above the 1.25 lenders want. Your payment alone is 13% of cash flow, well under the one-third ceiling. This deal breathes. Illustrative numbers, not a quote.

The SBA changed the rules — know them before you negotiate

A lot of your buyers will show up with an SBA 7(a) loan — 78% of surveyed buyers expect to use SBA financing. Since the SBA's SOP 50 10 8 took effect in June 2025, the fine print matters to you, the seller, directly.

Buyers must inject at least 10% of the total project cost. Your seller note can count toward that injection, but only if it sits on full standby for the life of the SBA loan — typically ten years of no principal and no interest paid to you, even if interest accrues on paper. And a standby note can cover at most half the injection; the buyer still has to bring at least 5% in genuine cash. The old trick where a note came off standby after 24 months is gone.

Practical translation: if a buyer asks you to carry 5% "on standby," understand you're agreeing to wait a decade for that money. Some sellers accept it to get a deal done at full price. That's a legitimate choice. Just price it like the long, unsecured loan it is, and don't let anyone present it as a minor detail.

How you protect yourself

Sellers get burned by sloppy paper far more often than by bad luck. The standard kit: a promissory note with default, late-payment, and acceleration clauses; a personal guarantee from the buyer (and often their spouse); and a UCC lien on the business assets, so if things go wrong you have a claim on something real. Many sellers also require ongoing financial statements and life insurance on the buyer for the note balance. If a bank is in the deal, your lien will sit second to theirs. Accept that, but get it in writing rather than by surprise.

The single best protection isn't a clause at all. It's the down payment. Morgan & Westfield's guidance pegs typical down payments at 30% to 80%, and warns that below 30% the default risk climbs fast. A buyer with serious money at risk finds a way to make the Tuesday payroll. A buyer with 5% in can walk away from your note the way people walk away from gym memberships.

One more quiet benefit: an installment sale can spread your capital gains tax across the years you receive payments instead of piling it into one, per IRS Publication 537. Run it past your CPA before you assume it helps in your bracket.

When to say no

Seller financing isn't a virtue. It's a tool, and there are deals where the right answer is no: a buyer with no relevant experience and thin reserves, a buyer who bristles at a personal guarantee, or a business whose cash flow barely clears the debt at 1.1× coverage. If the deal only closes because you absorbed all the risk the bank refused, the bank was telling you something.

This is also a spot where a good broker earns their fee — structuring the note, vetting the buyer's finances, and refusing to let a weak buyer hide behind your generosity. FAQ

Is seller financing a good idea when selling a business?

Usually, in moderation. Financed deals tend to sell for 20% to 30% more, and about 80% of small business sales include some seller financing. Carry 10% to 20% with real security — note, personal guarantee, UCC lien — rather than half the price on a handshake.

What interest rate should I charge on a seller note?

The going range has been 6% to 8%, over three to seven years, priced to the buyer's risk rather than to bank rates. The exception is an SBA full-standby note, which typically pays you nothing until the SBA loan is retired even if interest accrues.

What happens if the buyer stops paying?

You enforce the note: acceleration clause, personal guarantee, and your UCC lien on the assets, behind the bank's lien if there is one. Which is why the buyer's down payment and the coverage ratio are your real protection, not the paperwork alone.
