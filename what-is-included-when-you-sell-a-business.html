Do You Keep the Cash When You Sell Your Business? What's Included in the Sale

By The Broker Index Team · October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Usually, yes — you keep the cash. The standard structure for a small-business sale is cash-free, debt-free: the seller keeps the cash in the bank and the accounts receivable, pays off the business's debts at or before closing, and delivers the operating assets — equipment, inventory, the name, the phone number, the customer relationships, the goodwill — free and clear. The headline price buys the machine, not the money already inside it. But "usually" is doing work in that sentence, because the purchase agreement's included-and-excluded asset lists are what actually control, and the differences between deals hide real dollars.

What conveys, what doesn't

In a typical asset sale, included: furniture, fixtures and equipment; a normal level of inventory; the trade name and goodwill; customer lists and records; the phone numbers, website, and social accounts; transferable permits; and assignment of the lease and key contracts (which need consents of their own). Excluded: cash and bank accounts, accounts receivable earned before closing, personal vehicles and personal items, and — on the other side of the ledger — the debts, which are yours to clear. The company entity itself stays with you too; the buyer is purchasing assets out of it, which is the heart of the asset-vs-stock-sale choice.

The expensive misunderstandings live in the gray zones. Is the delivery van a business asset or your car? Is the beer license transferable or a new application? Does "inventory included" mean the normal $40K on the shelves or the $90K you stockpiled when a vendor ran a deal? Write the lists like a stranger will read them adversarially, because one will.

Receivables: the $300,000 lesson

Keeping your receivables sounds unambiguous until you see what it does to price in edge cases. BizBuySell's Learning Center runs a clean example: a $2M business carrying $300,000 of accounts receivable. If the seller keeps the A/R — standard — the buyer must fund that much working capital from day one and will discount their offer accordingly; if the A/R conveys, the price should rise to match. Either answer can be right. The wrong answer is discovering at the closing table that you and the buyer assumed differently about a six-figure line item.

Inventory gets the same treatment at smaller scale. The convention, per Viking Mergers: define "normal" inventory as a 12-month normalized average, count it at closing, and true up the price for the difference. Work-in-progress gets valued by percentage of completion. None of this is hard. All of it has to be written down.

From headline price to wire: the waterfall

Worked example (hypothetical)

Marcus sells his plumbing supply business for a headline $650,000, cash-free debt-free. At closing, the money moves like this:

Price $650,000
+ cash he keeps: $30,000
+ receivables he keeps and collects: $45,000
− truck loan paid off at closing: $25,000
− accounts payable he settles: $20,000
− broker commission (10%): $65,000
− legal fees: $10,000

Net to Marcus: $650,000 + $30,000 + $45,000 − $25,000 − $20,000 − $65,000 − $10,000 = $605,000 — before taxes, which are their own article. The headline said $650K. The honest number was $605K, and Marcus knew it three months in advance because somebody built this waterfall before he signed anything. Build yours early.

Bigger deals add a working capital peg

Above Main Street size, buyers stop letting sellers strip every dollar of working capital. The deal includes a peg: a required level of working capital — receivables plus inventory minus payables — usually set from a trailing 12- or 6-month average, per Schneider Downs. Deliver the business with less and the price adjusts down dollar-for-dollar; more, and it adjusts up. The true-up typically lands 60 to 90 days after closing, and an escrow often holds the adjustment money — one of several uses of the escrow holdback. If your buyer's lawyer says "peg" and your lawyer doesn't flinch, you've hired the right one.

One more line worth reading twice: the purchase price allocation. Buyer and seller must file matching IRS Form 8594 schedules carving the price across asset classes, and the carve changes both parties' taxes. It's negotiated, not clerical.

How to not get surprised

Three habits cover nearly everything. First, build the included/excluded lists in writing at the letter-of-intent stage, not during closing week. Second, run the full waterfall — price, plus what you keep, minus what you owe, minus fees — the day you get an offer, so every later conversation is about a real number. Third, put inventory and receivables conventions in the LOI itself: whether inventory is in or on top, how it's counted, who keeps which receivables, and who handles warranty claims on pre-closing work. Deals rarely die over these items. They just quietly transfer five figures to whichever side wrote the first draft.

The gray-zone checklist worth an hour

Before the LOI, walk the business with a notepad and sort every ambiguous item into a column. Vehicles: titled to the company or to you, and which conveys? Equipment on lease rather than owned — the buyer is assuming those payments, and they belong in the disclosure. Licenses and permits: transferable, or new applications with waiting periods (liquor licenses being the classic deal-timeline surprise)? Deposits — utility, lease, vendor — are routinely forgotten money; decide who gets them back. Prepaid expenses like insurance and subscriptions either convey with a credit or get prorated at closing. Customer deposits and gift cards cut the other way: that's cash you've collected for work the buyer must deliver, so expect a price adjustment. Loyalty points, warranty obligations, and the family photos on the office wall round out the list. None of these items is large. Unlisted, each one is an argument scheduled for the worst possible week.

Frequently asked questions

Do I keep the cash in the bank when I sell?

Usually yes — cash-free, debt-free is the default. You keep cash and receivables, clear the debts, and deliver the operating assets. The agreement's asset lists control, so read them.

Is inventory included in the price?

Deal-specific. Many listings price inventory separately at cost; others include a normal level, defined by a 12-month average and trued up at closing. Nail it down in the LOI.

What's a working capital peg?

A required working-capital level on larger deals, set from a trailing average. Deliver less, the price drops; more, it rises — settled in a true-up 60–90 days post-closing.
