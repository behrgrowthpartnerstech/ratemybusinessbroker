How Do You Check a Business Broker's References? The Three Calls Worth Six Figures

By The Broker Index Team · October 31, 2026 · 9 min read

Ask for the sellers from the broker's last three closed deals — the last three, so they can't hand you their three happiest clients from 2019 — plus one seller whose listing expired unsold, plus one professional who's worked a deal with them. Then actually call. Most sellers never do, which is remarkable given the stakes: by common industry estimates only 20–30% of listings ever sell, and the single biggest controllable variable is the person running the process. An hour of calls is the cheapest diligence in the entire transaction, and the math below says it's worth six figures in expected value.

Why "last three," specifically

The phrasing comes from buyer-side diligence practice — Baton's broker-vetting guide makes it a rule: the last three transactions, not three transactions. A curated reference list is a marketing asset; a chronological one is a record. The broker who bristles at the distinction has told you something. While you're asking, get the fuller ledger too: the last ten listings with outcomes. Sold, expired, withdrawn — and the sold-to-asking ratio on the closes. Market context for that ratio: businesses under $1M currently sell at 93.6% of asking. A broker consistently closing at 75% of ask is overpricing listings to win them; one at 98% might be underpricing to churn them. Both patterns show up in exactly this data and nowhere else.

The questions that actually discriminate

Reference calls fail when they invite pleasantries. These nine questions don't. For the closed sellers: What did it list for, and what did it close at? How long did it take? How often did the broker update you without being chased? Who actually found the buyer? What surprised you at closing? For the expired-listing seller: What reason did the broker give? Do you believe it now? For everyone, the two closers: Would you use them again? — and the one that catches polite liars — Have you referred anyone to them? "Yes, my brother-in-law" is a reference. A pause is a review.

The professional call — a deal attorney, CPA, or SBA lender who's closed with them — checks a different axis: paperwork quality, responsiveness under deadline, honesty when a deal wobbled. Professionals are also likelier to be candid, since they're not grading their own purchase decision.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Hana is choosing between two brokers for her $450,000 listing at a 10% fee. Reference calls suggest Broker A prices honestly and closes; Broker B collects listings and lets them age. Model it as expected-value arithmetic — the probabilities are Hana's judgment after the calls, not statistics, but they discipline the decision. Baseline close odds for any listing run 20–30%.

Broker A: sells at 96% of ask = $432,000; after the 10% fee, $388,800; at a 0.79 chance of closing, expected value ≈ $307,152.

Broker B: overprices then cuts, selling at 88% = $396,000; after fee, $356,400; at a 0.25 chance of closing, expected value ≈ $89,100.

Gap: roughly $218,000 — produced by one hour of phone calls that cost nothing. Even if you think the probability spread is half as wide, the hour still pays like a quarter's revenue.

Reading between the lines on the calls

Reference-call answers come in code, and the code is learnable. "He worked really hard" without a number attached usually means the result disappointed. "She was always available" is strong — availability is the first thing that dies in a listing-collector's practice. Listen for unprompted specifics: a seller who volunteers "we got $12K over ask because she ran two buyers against each other" is describing something that happened; a seller who speaks only in adjectives may be describing something they hoped for. Ask every reference what they would do differently, which politely invites criticism without demanding disloyalty. And weigh the expired-listing reference heaviest of all: how a broker behaves when a deal is dying — communication, honesty about price, graceful exit — is the single best preview of the service you'll get in the hard months, because the easy months don't need a broker at all.

One caution in the other direction: a single bitter reference doesn't end a candidacy. Deals die for reasons brokers don't control, and some sellers blame the messenger for a price the market rejected. You're reading patterns across five calls, not prosecuting one anecdote. Two references describing the same failure — vanishing after the listing went live, say — is a pattern. One seller still angry about an appraisal is a Tuesday.

Timing matters too: make the calls after the broker's pitch but before you sign anything, while your memory of their claims is fresh enough to check against. A pitch that said "I average 95% of asking" should survive contact with three sellers who remember their own numbers.

Beyond the calls: the paper trail

References are one leg. The others take twenty minutes at a keyboard. Run the broker through FINRA BrokerCheck if they touch securities-side deals or carry an M&A advisor pedigree. Verify any claimed credential — a CBI is checkable in the IBBA's member directory, and "certified" claims that can't be verified are their own answer. Search their name plus "lawsuit" and check county court records; one dispute means they've been in business, a pattern means they'll be in business with you. Then read independent reviews — not the testimonial page they wrote, the reviews they can't edit. Work through a structured list like Clearly Acquired's ten questions alongside our own eight questions to ask before signing.

Finally, read the listing agreement before any reference glow convinces you to skip it — the fee trigger, the tail, the termination clause. Frequently asked questions

What references should I ask a business broker for?

The sellers from their last three closings, one seller whose listing expired unsold, and one deal professional — attorney, CPA, or lender — who has worked a transaction with them.

What questions should I ask a broker's references?

List-versus-close price, time on market, unprompted communication frequency, who found the buyer, closing surprises — and the two closers: would you use them again, and have you actually referred anyone?

How else can I verify a business broker's track record?

Last ten listings with outcomes and sold-to-asking ratio, FINRA BrokerCheck, credential verification with the IBBA, county court records, and independent reviews rather than the testimonials page.
