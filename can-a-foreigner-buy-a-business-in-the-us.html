Can a Foreigner Buy a Business in the U.S.? The 2026 Rules

By The Broker Index Team · November 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes — there is no citizenship or residency requirement to own a U.S. business, and there never has been. A buyer from anywhere can form an LLC, get an EIN, and close. The two real hurdles are different ones. First, work authorization: owning a business doesn't let you work in it. Second, financing: since March 1, 2026, SBA loans require 100% of direct and indirect owners to be U.S. citizens or nationals primarily residing in the United States. So foreign buyers pay cash, lean on seller notes, or come through the E-2 and EB-5 investor-visa routes. Here's the whole map.

Owning is the easy part

LLCs and C corporations are open to any nationality (the S corporation is the exception — it requires U.S. persons as shareholders). You can own from abroad, hire a U.S. manager, and collect distributions as a passive owner without any visa at all. What you can't do without work authorization is stand behind the counter and run the place. That's the fork in the road: passive ownership with a manager, or an investor visa that authorizes you to operate — and the manager route has its own math, which our absentee-ownership guide runs in detail.

The SBA door closed in March

The history moved fast. For decades, businesses 51%+ owned by citizens, nationals, or green-card holders qualified for SBA loans. In mid-2025 that tightened to 100% of those three groups. Then, effective March 1, 2026, the rule tightened again: citizens and nationals only, primarily residing in the U.S. — NerdWallet's coverage and the SBA's procedural notice have the specifics. Lawful permanent residents, visa holders, and DACA recipients can't own any percentage of an SBA-financed business; even a U.S. citizen whose principal residence is abroad trips the rule. It covers 7(a) and 504, extended to microloans and surety bonds from April 1. Existing loans are unaffected.

Two footnotes from Windsor Advantage's technical guide: an ineligible owner can divest before the loan number is issued — a real, documented sale, not a wink — and putting a citizen's name on the cap table as a front is a false statement on a federal loan application, which is the kind of shortcut that ends in a courtroom rather than a closing.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Lena, a German designer, wants a café priced at $280,000 with $120,000 of SDE — about a 2.3× multiple. Her citizen rival finances it: project $312,000 ($280K + $20K working capital + $12K closing), injection $31,200, loan $280,800 at 10% over 10 years = $3,711 a month ($44,532 a year). Cash available: $120,000 − $50,000 living = $70,000 → coverage 1.57. He brings $31,200 to the table.

Lena can't touch that loan. Her E-2 route wants personal, at-risk funds — and loans secured by the business itself don't count. So she wires the full $280,000 through an escrow conditioned on visa approval. Same café. Roughly nine times the cash.

The E-2: buy the business, earn the right to run it

The E-2 treaty-investor visa has no statutory minimum. The test is proportionality: the investment must be "substantial" relative to the business, which for a purchase means covering most of the price — the National Law Review's 2026 requirements guide shows a $200K business wanting $150K+ invested. Practitioners' realistic floor for a purchase runs about $100,000–$150,000. Funds must be at risk and irrevocably committed; the standard structure is escrow released on visa approval, so a denial returns the money. Loans count only when secured by your personal assets, never by the business you're buying. The business can't be marginal — it has to support more than just you. The fine print that matters: it's available to citizens of 80+ treaty countries, runs up to five years per issuance and renews indefinitely, your spouse gets work authorization — and it is not a green card, ever.

The EB-5: a green card, at a different altitude

EB-5 is the immigration-through-investment route: $1,050,000, or $800,000 in a targeted employment area or infrastructure project, plus the creation of ten full-time jobs — figures per the Congressional Research Service, with CPI adjustments every five years starting January 1, 2027. The catch for business buyers: buying a business that already employs ten people isn't creating ten jobs. EB-5 buyers build or expand. The median Main Street listing doesn't fit the frame.

How these deals actually fund

Cash, most often — wired through escrow with documented source of funds. Seller notes fill gaps: typically 15–30% of price on smaller deals, which makes the seller the lender, with everything that implies about vetting the buyer and structuring the note per our seller-financing guide. Conventional bank loans without SBA backing exist but are rare at Main Street size — banks that lend at this scale mostly lend through the SBA programs the rule just closed.

If you're the seller

The March rule reshaped your buyer pool: green-card holders and visa holders can no longer bring SBA money to your deal, full stop. But an all-cash foreign buyer with documented funds is one of the cleanest closings there is — no bank, no appraisal condition, no 60-day underwriting. The move is asking two questions early, politely: what's your status, and how will you fund? It's the same qualification logic as spotting a serious buyer, applied before you've burned a month. And if you're the buyer, the listings don't care about your passport — start at where businesses are listed, and read the SBA guide if your cap table qualifies.

Frequently asked questions

Can a foreigner buy a business in the USA?

Yes — ownership has no citizenship requirement. The hurdles are work authorization to run it and financing, since SBA loans now require all-citizen ownership.

Can a green-card holder get an SBA loan?

No. Since March 1, 2026, every direct and indirect owner must be a U.S. citizen or national primarily residing in the U.S. LPRs can manage at zero ownership; existing loans are unaffected.

How much does an E-2 visa investment need to be?

No fixed minimum — "substantial" relative to the business, usually most of the purchase price, with ~$100–150K the practical floor. Funds must be personal and at risk; escrow conditioned on approval is standard.
