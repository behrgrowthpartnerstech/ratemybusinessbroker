How Do I Buy Out My Business Partner? Price, Paperwork, and the New SBA Rules

By The Broker Index Team · October 11, 2026 · 8 min read

Buying out a business partner takes three things: a price you can both defend, money to pay it, and paperwork that actually ends the relationship. The price should come from an independent valuation, not a number someone remembers agreeing to years ago. The money usually comes from an SBA 7(a) loan, a seller note from the departing partner, or both. And the paperwork has to transfer equity, release guarantees, and close every side door. Since October 1, 2026, the SBA treats partner buyouts as their own deal category with their own rules — some of them friendlier than before, some stricter. Here's the whole map.

Step one: get a price neither of you picked

Most partner buyouts go sideways at the price, because both sides have spent years quietly deciding what the business is worth, and the numbers never match. The fix is boring: hand it to a third party. If you have a buy-sell agreement, read it first — many fix a valuation formula or name an appraisal process, and courts tend to hold partners to what they signed. No agreement? Commission an independent valuation. If SBA money is involved you'll need one anyway, since the SBA requires an independent valuation on any change of ownership.

For context on what valuations run on: Main Street businesses price off seller's discretionary earnings. The Q3 2026 market median was a $350,000 sale price at an average 2.30× multiple for businesses under $1M, per the BizBuySell Insight Report. Your partner's half isn't automatically half the headline number, either — minority stakes carry discounts, which is a fight worth having with an appraiser in the room. (If you're on the other end of this, selling a piece rather than buying one, see selling part of your business.)

The new SBA rules, in plain English

SOP 50 10 8.1 took effect October 1, 2026, and it carved partner buyouts into their own category — the SBA calls them owner buyouts. The rules that matter, per Starfield & Smith's lender guidance:

  • Someone has to stay. At least one original owner must remain in the business and personally guarantee the loan — regardless of how small their retained stake is.
  • History, not hope. Debt service coverage must hit 1.25× on the last fiscal year or a two-year average of actual results. Projections can't be used, period. Your rosy year-three forecast is worth exactly nothing here.
  • Outsiders stay under half. An individual not currently employed by the business may buy in below 50% — and can't become the largest shareholder. Full takeovers by outsiders run through the standard change-of-ownership lane instead.
  • 10% equity injection. Standard, though the lender can reduce or waive it with documented liquidity and a non-negative net worth on the business. Seller standby debt can cover up to half of any required injection.
  • No quality of earnings requirement. Owner buyouts are exempt from the $3M+ QoE rule that now applies to regular acquisitions.

One more wrinkle for deals already running on IOUs: if your partner financed you informally and you want to refinance that note into an SBA loan later, the note now needs 36 months of seasoning — up from 24. Paper your interim arrangements like they'll be audited, because eventually they will be.

A 50/50 buyout, worked end to end

Worked example (hypothetical)

Dana and Marco own a commercial landscaping company 50/50. An independent valuation puts the business at $700,000. Marco wants out; his half is $350,000. Dana borrows the full $350,000 through a 7(a) loan at 10% over 10 years: $4,625 a month, $55,503 a year in debt service.

The business throws off $150,000 in documented cash flow after Dana's salary. Coverage: $150,000 ÷ $55,503 = 2.70× — comfortably past the 1.25× bar, and it's all on last year's actuals, which is what the rule demands.

Now the cautionary version. Suppose the actuals showed only $20,000 because the partners had been splitting distributions and the "real" profitability only shows up in Dana's projections. $20,000 ÷ $55,503 = 0.36×. Declined — and under 8.1, no amount of forecasting fixes it. The business has to demonstrate the cash flow before the buyout, not promise it after.

If the bank isn't the answer: seller notes and installments

Plenty of partner buyouts never touch a bank. The departing partner carries a note — say, five years of monthly payments at a market rate — and the business cash-flows the exit. It's faster and cheaper, but understand what your partner is accepting: they've become your lender, with all the default risk that implies. Protections that make a note carryable: a personal guarantee, a security interest in the equity being sold, and acceleration if payments slip. (The seller's side of this trade is covered in our seller financing guide.)

Hybrid structures are common too: SBA loan for most of it, with the departing partner's standby note covering up to half of the equity injection. Just remember the standby part is real — no payments while the SBA loan is outstanding, which some departing partners discover with visible disappointment at the closing table.

The paperwork that actually ends it

Equity changes hands through a redemption (the company buys the shares back) or a cross-purchase (you buy them directly) — your CPA will care which, because the tax treatment differs. Beyond the purchase agreement itself, the closing stack should include: resignations from every officer and director role, releases running both directions, removal of the departing partner from bank accounts and credit lines, and — the one people forget — their release from personal guarantees on the lease, the line of credit, and any vendor accounts. A partner who's been bought out but still guarantees your lease isn't actually gone; they're just unpaid.

This is a bring-a-lawyer transaction even when it's friendly. Especially when it's friendly, since friendly deals are the ones where nobody reads anything. If the buyout is really a family succession in disguise, the employee and family sale rules — gift tax, applicable federal rates, the works — apply on top.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get an SBA loan to buy out my business partner?

Yes. Owner buyouts are their own category under SOP 50 10 8.1. The remaining owner stays and personally guarantees, coverage must hit 1.25× on historical financials, and the 10% injection applies unless the lender documents a reason to reduce it.

How do you value a partner's share?

Buy-sell agreement first, independent valuation second. SBA deals require the valuation anyway. On Main Street that means SDE times a market multiple — currently averaging 2.30× under $1M — with a discount conversation for minority stakes.

Can an outside buyer purchase my partner's half?

With SBA money, only below 50%, and they can't end up the largest shareholder. An outsider who wants half or more means a different structure — a full change of ownership or conventional financing.

Buyout first, sale later?
