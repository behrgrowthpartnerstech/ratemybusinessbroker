How Do I Prepare My Business for Sale? The 12-Month Plan

By The Broker Index Team · September 21, 2026 · 9 min read

Here's the short answer: start twelve months before you want to list, and spend that year doing four things — getting the books clean enough for a lender, documenting every add-back with receipts, making the business run without you, and getting a real valuation so you price it to survive SBA underwriting. The sale itself takes six to nine months. The price it closes at was mostly decided in the year before you listed.

Most owners don't do this. In the IBBA's Q1 2026 Market Pulse survey, 70% of owners selling businesses under $500K walked into the sale with no exit planning at all. And per BizBuySell's Q2 2026 Insight Report, only 14% of owners had ever completed a professional valuation — 35% admitted they had no idea what their business was worth. That's the competition. Being prepared is rarer than being profitable.

Why twelve months, specifically

Because the clock is longer than you think. IBBA data puts listing-to-close at about 6 months for deals under $500K and 9 months for deals over $1M — and that's after your prep is done. Buyers and lenders will scrutinize your trailing twelve months of financials, so the year before you list is the year that gets bought. Fix something in month 11 and it barely shows up on paper. Fix it in month 1 and it's a full year of clean history by the time a buyer's accountant goes digging.

There's also a simpler reason. Rushed sellers accept whatever the books say. Prepared sellers get to change what the books say — legally, by running the business cleaner. For a sense of the full timeline once you do list, we've broken it down in How Long Does It Take to Sell a Business?

Months 12–9: Clean the books and document your add-backs

Small-business value is priced off seller's discretionary earnings — SDE — which is your net profit plus your salary plus the personal and one-time expenses you legitimately ran through the business. The catch is the word "legitimately." A buyer will only credit add-backs you can prove, and an SBA lender is stricter still. Every add-back needs a paper trail: a W-2, an invoice, a policy statement.

So the first quarter of your prep year looks like this. Get your bookkeeping current and on accrual-consistent P&Ls. Stop running personal expenses through the business — yes, it saves taxes; it also erases value at 2.7 times the amount, as the math below shows. Build a written add-back schedule with a receipt behind every line. And if any meaningful chunk of your revenue is unreported cash, understand that it simply doesn't exist at sale time. Nobody pays for income that isn't on a tax return.

Worked example (hypothetical): what documentation is worth

A landscaping company's tax return shows $92,000 in net income. The owner's documented add-backs: her W-2 salary of $65,000, employer-paid health insurance of $9,000, a one-time $5,400 website rebuild, and a $3,600 truck lease that's genuinely personal — all with paperwork.

SDE = $92,000 + $65,000 + $9,000 + $5,400 + $3,600 = $175,000

At the market's 2.7× average multiple: $175,000 × 2.7 = $472,500 asking range.

She also ran about $14,000 of other personal spending through the business over the years — but kept no receipts and can't tie it to anything. Buyers won't credit it. At 2.7×, that missing shoebox of receipts costs her $14,000 × 2.7 = $37,800 off the price. Documentation isn't paperwork; it's price.

Months 9–6: Fire yourself (gradually)

The second-biggest discount in Main Street deals, after messy books, is owner dependence. If every customer relationship, supplier password, and pricing decision lives in your head, a buyer isn't purchasing a business — they're purchasing a job with a scary mortgage. So spend this quarter making yourself less necessary. Write down how the work actually gets done. Cross-train a second-in-command. Move customer relationships onto the company, not your cell phone. Take a two-week vacation and see what breaks; that's your to-do list.

This is also the quarter to fix transferability landmines: a lease with less than three years of term left (SBA lenders want lease term to cover the loan), customer contracts that terminate on change of ownership, and any licenses that can't be assigned. Each of those can stall a closing for months if you find them late.

Months 6–3: Price it like a lender would

Now get a real valuation — from a broker, an appraiser, or at minimum a serious market-comp analysis. Not because the number will be fun (it often isn't), but because 78% of buyers in BizBuySell's Q2 2026 survey expect to use SBA financing, which means your price has to survive a bank's math, not just a buyer's optimism.

The number that matters is the debt-service coverage ratio: cash flow available divided by annual loan payments. Most lenders want roughly 1.25, and the SBA floor is 1.15. On our example deal — $472,500 price, 10% down, the ~$425,000 balance on a 10-year SBA 7(a) note at around 10.5% — annual debt service runs about $68,800. SDE of $175,000 ÷ $68,800 is a coverage ratio near 2.5, comfortably bankable. The deals that die in underwriting are the ones priced so high, or with SDE so soft, that the ratio slips under 1.25. See the SBA's 7(a) program for current terms, and our guide to reasonable multiples for where your industry lands.

One more reason to know your number early: size changes the multiple. Bigger earnings don't just multiply the price — they raise the multiplier itself.

Months 3–0: Build the team, then keep your foot on the gas

The last quarter is about people. Line up a deal attorney and a CPA who've done business sales before — your cousin who does real estate closings doesn't count. Interview two or three brokers, and check them the way you'd check a contractor: ask what they've actually sold in your size range and industry, how they'll market confidentially, and exactly how their fee works (our piece on how brokers get paid covers the standard structures). Read their client reviews on The Broker Index before you sign anything.

And through all of it: keep running the business like you're keeping it. Buyers pay for the trend line. A seller who mentally checks out at month three hands the buyer a declining trailing-twelve-months and an invitation to retrade the price. The cruel irony of selling a business is that the best time to look like you don't need to sell is the whole time you're selling.

The pre-listing checklist

  • Financials: three years of tax returns and P&Ls, current interim statements, and a documented add-back schedule that matches them.
  • Operations: written procedures, a trained second-in-command, and customer relationships that survive your exit.
  • Transferability: assignable lease with adequate term, contracts without change-of-control tripwires, licenses sorted.
  • Price: a valuation you can defend, at a number that clears a 1.25 debt-service coverage ratio for an SBA buyer.
  • Team: deal-experienced attorney and CPA, and a broker you vetted rather than the first one who mailed you a postcard.

FAQ

How far in advance should I prepare my business for sale?

At least twelve months before listing. The sale itself runs 6–9 months per IBBA data, and the improvements that raise your price need a full tax year on paper before a buyer's accountant will believe them.

What documents do I need before selling my business?

Three years of tax returns and P&Ls, interim financials, a receipts-backed add-back schedule, your lease, key contracts, an equipment list, and payroll records. Every one of them gets requested in due diligence; having them ready is what keeps a deal on schedule.

Should I stop investing in the business once I decide to sell?

No. Buyers price the trailing twelve months and the direction it's heading. Coasting into your listing shrinks both the multiple and the buyer pool, and declining final-year revenue is a classic trigger for a price retrade.
