How Much Tax Will I Pay When I Sell My Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 18, 2026 · 9 min read

For most owners selling a pass-through business, the federal tax bill lands between 15% and 25% of the gain — but there is no single "business sale tax rate," and anyone who quotes you one is rounding away real money. An asset sale gets carved into classes on IRS Form 8594, and each class has its own treatment: goodwill at long-term capital gains, equipment gain at ordinary rates to the extent of past depreciation, inventory at essentially nothing if sold at cost. Your bill is the weighted average of a negotiation most sellers don't realize is happening. Here's the whole map, worked on a real-sized deal.

The rates in play for 2026

Long-term capital gains: 0%, 15%, or 20% — for single filers in 2026, the 15% bracket runs to $545,500 of taxable income ($613,700 married filing jointly), per the published 2026 brackets, so most Main Street sellers live at 15% for the capital-gain slice. High earners add the 3.8% net investment income tax above $200K single / $250K MFJ of modified AGI — though NIIT generally doesn't apply to gain from a business you actively operated, one of the quieter wins of being an owner-operator rather than a passive holder. Ordinary income rates apply to depreciation recapture under §1245 — uncapped — and unrecaptured §1250 gain on real property is capped at 25%. State tax stacks on top of all of it, from zero to double digits depending on your address.

A $600,000 sale, line by line

Worked example (hypothetical)

Nadia sells her catering company's assets for $600,000. The Form 8594 allocation: inventory $40,000, equipment $180,000, goodwill $380,000.

Inventory: sold at cost — no gain, no tax. Equipment: fully depreciated over the years, so the entire $180,000 is §1245 recapture at her ordinary rate of 24%: $43,200. Goodwill: long-term capital gain at 15%: $57,000.

Total federal bill: $43,200 + $57,000 = $100,200 — about 17% of the price. Now the negotiation hiding inside it: the buyer wants dollars shifted from goodwill (which they amortize over 15 years) into equipment (which they can depreciate fast). Every $100,000 that moves from Nadia's 15% goodwill to her 24% recapture costs her 9 points — $9,000 — while helping the buyer. Neither side is wrong to push. The seller who doesn't know the fight exists loses it by default.

Structure moves the bill more than any deduction

Asset vs. stock. Buyers prefer asset deals for the depreciation restart; sellers prefer stock deals for clean capital-gain treatment. The after-tax swing between structures runs 10–15% of the price on many deals — the full argument lives in asset sale vs. stock sale. C-corporation owners face the double-tax problem on asset sales (corporate tax at 21%, then tax again on the distribution), which is why C-corp sellers and their CPAs start planning years before listing, and why personal-goodwill arguments and S-elections exist.

Installment sales. Carry a seller note and §453 lets you recognize capital gain as payments arrive — useful for staying inside the 15% bracket. Two traps, per Instead's §453 guidance: all depreciation recapture is taxed in the year of sale, cash collected or not, and you're holding buyer default risk while the IRS holds your receipt. QSBS. If you happen to hold qualifying C-corp stock under §1202, recent law allows 50/75/100% exclusions at 3/4/5-year holds on post-July-2025 stock, with caps of $15M or 10× basis — narrow, spectacular when it fits.

The covenant line. Dollars allocated to your non-compete are ordinary income; goodwill is capital gain. Small line, real money.

Both sides file the same form — so negotiate it like a price term

Form 8594 requires buyer and seller to report matching allocations, per Young and the Invested's walkthrough, which means the allocation gets settled in the purchase agreement, not in April. Treat it as part of the price: a deal at $590,000 with a seller-friendly allocation can net you more than $600,000 with a buyer-friendly one. Your CPA should see the draft allocation before you sign the LOI — the moment of maximum leverage — and your attorney should confirm the agreement language matches the schedule. Mismatched 8594s are an audit invitation addressed to both parties; Alpine Mar's structuring guidance covers the C-corp cases where getting this wrong compounds into true double-digit damage.

Timing is the last lever: a December versus January closing moves the entire gain across a tax year, which matters if your other income swings or brackets shift. It's a routine conversation to have in October and an impossible one to have retroactively. None of this is exotic planning — it's reading one schedule carefully, early, with professionals who've seen it before.

The pre-sale tax calendar

Tax planning for a sale rewards lead time the way nothing else in the deal does. Two to three years out: confirm your entity structure is the one you want to sell from — C-corp owners weighing an S-election need years for the built-in-gains clock, and that conversation can't start too early. One year out: stop running personal expenses through the business (they muddy both your SDE and your allocation story), and have your CPA rough out the bill under an asset sale at your likely price, so the number in your head is after-tax from the start. At LOI: negotiate the allocation schedule and the covenant line before exclusivity removes your leverage. At closing quarter: decide December versus January with actual numbers, and set aside the recapture cash if you're carrying a note — that tax arrives in April whether or not the buyer's payments do. Sellers who run this calendar routinely keep several points more of the price than sellers who meet their tax bill as a surprise.

Frequently asked questions

How much tax will I pay when I sell?

Typically 15–25% federal, as a blend: goodwill at capital gains (15% for most), equipment recapture at ordinary rates, inventory at cost tax-free, plus possible 3.8% NIIT and state tax. The allocation sets your blend.

What is depreciation recapture?

Prior depreciation clawed back at ordinary rates under §1245, uncapped — and in an installment sale it's all due in year one, even before the cash arrives.

Does seller financing reduce the tax?

It spreads capital gain across years under §453 and can hold you in lower brackets — but recapture stays in year one, and you carry the buyer's default risk.
