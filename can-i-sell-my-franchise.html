Can I Sell My Franchise? How Franchise Resales Actually Work

By The Broker Index Team · October 22, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes — you can sell your franchise, and thousands of owners do it every year. Franchise Item 20 tables treat roughly 5% of units transferring annually as a healthy system. But a franchise resale isn't a normal business sale, because there's a third party at the table who didn't put in a dime and still holds most of the cards: the franchisor. They approve your buyer, they often get first crack at buying the business themselves, they charge a transfer fee, and they decide when your buyer is trained enough to take the keys. None of that makes the sale impossible. It makes the sale a process, and sellers who learn the process before listing keep more money and lose fewer buyers.

The three gates every franchise resale passes through

Gate one is the right of first refusal. Most franchise agreements give the franchisor a window, typically 15–60 days, to step in and buy your business on the same terms your buyer offered. They rarely exercise it, but the clock runs before your deal can proceed, so build it into the timeline. Some agreements also treat partial equity transfers as a "sale" — triggers commonly sit at 10–25% of ownership — so even bringing in a partner can wake the clause up.

Gate two is buyer approval. Your buyer applies to the franchisor like any new franchisee: financial pre-qualification usually asks for $250K–$1M in net worth, $75K–$300K liquid, and a credit score around 680+, and takes 14–30 days. Formal approval runs 60–120 days. The franchisor can say no, and if they do, your deal dies no matter how much your buyer loves the business. Screen for the brand's minimums before you accept an offer — our guide to spotting serious buyers applies double here.

Gate three is the transfer fee and conditions. The fee itself is usually flat: Subway runs about $7,500, McDonald's $7,500–$15,000, The UPS Store $7,500–$10,000, Five Guys $15,000, Dunkin' about $25,000, and territory-based systems like Servpro can reach $25K–$50K. The expensive part often isn't the fee — it's the conditions attached. Franchisors routinely require the unit be brought up to current image standards before or shortly after transfer, and a remodel can run $50K–$300K. Who eats that cost is a negotiation between you and your buyer, so settle it in the letter of intent, not at closing week.

What a franchise resale is actually worth

Franchise resales price like other Main Street businesses: a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings, adjusted for the brand and the lease. Single-unit quick-service restaurants typically trade at 2.0–3.5× SDE, casual dining 1.5–3.0×, and service franchises 2.5–4.0×. Multi-unit packages of three or more stores graduate to EBITDA pricing at roughly 3.5–5.5×. For context, the broad market median across all small businesses is a $350,000 sale price at about 2.30× cash flow.

One quirk works in a buyer's favor and therefore in yours as a seller, if you frame it right: resales usually change hands at 30–50% below what it costs to build a new unit from scratch once you count construction, fees, and the losses of the ramp-up years. You're not selling a license. You're selling a unit that already has customers, staff, and a P&L. Worked example (hypothetical)

Dev owns a single sandwich franchise doing $120,000 of SDE and agrees to sell at $338,000 (about 2.8×). The waterfall: $338,000 price − $33,800 broker fee (10%) − $10,000 attorney and closing costs − $7,500 franchisor transfer fee = $286,700 in Dev's pocket, about 85 cents of every dollar.

Now the version nobody budgets for: the franchisor conditions approval on a refresh of the dining room. If the $60,000 remodel lands on Dev instead of the buyer, his 85 cents becomes 67. That one clause is worth more than every other negotiation in the deal — which is why it belongs in the LOI.

Sweeteners, and when the franchisor actually helps

Franchisors want units in strong hands, and the good ones grease transfers rather than block them. The most common sweetener is a royalty waiver or reduction for the incoming owner, typically 12–24 months, which effectively raises the buyer's first-year cash flow and makes your asking price easier to finance. Some brands maintain internal resale desks that match outgoing owners with vetted candidates. It costs nothing to ask what the brand will contribute. A franchisor that contributes nothing and demands a remodel is telling you something about the system your buyer is about to join — and franchising itself is still growing, with the IFA projecting about 845,000 franchise establishments in 2026, so motivated brands do compete for good operators.

Training is the last gate: 2–12 weeks for most systems, at a cost of $2K–$10K, sometimes before closing can even happen. And expect a transition period on your side too — a few weeks of handover is normal, and it's worth knowing how long sellers typically stay on before you promise anything.

Should you use a broker for a franchise resale?

A broker who has closed franchise resales in your brand, or at least your category, earns their fee twice here: once pricing the unit off real earnings instead of franchise-brochure math, and once shepherding the deal through the franchisor's gates without losing the buyer to a 120-day approval slog. Ask any candidate how many franchise transfers they've closed, which brands, and what the approval timeline was. If they can't answer, keep looking. Frequently asked questions

Can I sell my franchise to anyone I want?

No. The franchisor approves your buyer and usually holds a right of first refusal, a 15–60 day window to buy on the same terms. Your buyer must also meet the brand's financial minimums and complete training before closing.

How much does it cost to transfer a franchise?

Transfer fees run $5,000–$25,000 flat depending on the brand — Subway about $7,500, Dunkin' around $25,000. Budget separately for attorney fees, buyer training of $2K–$10K, and any required remodel, which can run $50K–$300K.

How long does it take to sell a franchise?

Typically 90–150 days from LOI to closing: 14–30 days of buyer pre-qualification, 60–120 days of formal approval, and 2–12 weeks of training, much of it overlapping.
