Do You Need a Business Broker Who Knows Your Industry?

By The Broker Index Team · October 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Sometimes. If your business is a straightforward local operation — a convenience store, a landscaping route — a competent generalist broker will do fine, because the buyer is a local individual and the valuation is standard SDE math. But if your business has a specific buyer pool, technical valuation drivers, or a sector premium to defend — auto repair, manufacturing, healthcare, HVAC, IT services — a genuine industry specialist can be worth six figures, because the gap between sector multiples is that wide. The catch: "industry specialist" is also the cheapest marketing claim in brokerage. This article covers when it matters and how to test it.

The multiple spread is the money

Closed-deal data from CT Acquisitions shows how far sector SDE multiples drift from the 2.58× all-industry average: Food & Restaurants at 2.27×, Health Care & Fitness at 2.72×, Manufacturing at 3.04×, Auto & Boat at 3.10×. BizBuySell's marketplace data tells the same story from another angle — restaurants trade at a median of just 1.85× SDE while the broad market averages 2.30×. A broker who doesn't know where your sector sits on that ladder either underprices you (you lose) or overprices you (nobody calls, and you lose slower).

Worked example (hypothetical)

Walt runs an auto-repair shop with $220,000 of SDE. A generalist prices it like an average small business at 2.6×: $572,000; after a 10% fee Walt nets $514,800. A real auto-sector specialist — who knows the consolidators buying shops, what equipment lists matter, and why the sector trades at 3.1× — gets $682,000; after the same 10% fee Walt nets $613,800. Difference: $99,000.

Now suppose the specialist charges 12% instead of 10%. Walt nets $682,000 × 0.88 = $600,160 — still $85,360 ahead of the generalist outcome. The fee debate is a rounding error next to the multiple debate. The expensive broker is the one who prices your sector wrong, whatever they charge. (How fees actually work: our fee guide.)

The counter-argument deserves a fair hearing

Some respected practitioners argue the opposite. Neumann & Associates' case against industry specialists runs: a narrow specialist may know too few buyers outside their lane, may be chummy with the same small circle of acquirers (whose interests are low prices, not yours), and may run a volume practice where your deal is one of thirty. There's truth here. A "specialist" whose buyer list is five consolidators he golfs with is an auctioneer with one bidder. What you actually want is process competence plus sector fluency — a broker who runs a wide, confidential search and can also speak your industry's language in the CIM and defend its multiple to lenders. One without the other leaks money.

When the specialist genuinely earns it

Industry expertise pays in four situations. Technical valuation drivers: a machine shop's price turns on equipment condition and customer concentration; a dental practice's on payor mix — things a generalist's template misses. Specific buyer pools: HVAC and plumbing are consolidating; a specialist knows which platforms are buying in your metro this quarter. Licensing and transfer complexity: liquor licenses, healthcare, anything with a franchisor in the middle. Sector premiums to defend: when your industry trades above average, the specialist's job is proving to buyers and lenders why — with comps, not adjectives.

For a main-street business without those features, hire the best process broker you can find and don't pay a specialist premium. Either way, the fundamentals of vetting apply: track record, references, and a listing agreement you've actually read. Where "specialist" goes wrong: three failure patterns

Pattern one is the website specialist — the generalist whose site has a page for every industry, each claiming deep expertise, all written the same afternoon. Harmless as marketing, expensive as a hiring decision. Pattern two is the captive specialist: genuinely expert, but their deal flow depends on two or three repeat acquirers, and repeat acquirers get repeat-customer treatment. Your listing becomes inventory for their best buyers rather than the subject of a real market process. Pattern three is the stale specialist, who dominated your sector a decade ago and still quotes 2015 multiples in either direction — underpricing a consolidating industry or overpricing a declining one. All three survive on the same fuel: sellers who accept the label instead of auditing the record.

The audit is quick. Closed deals in your sector in the last 24 months, not career totals. Multiples achieved against the current sector benchmarks above. Buyer diversity — if their last five sector deals went to the same acquirer, ask why, and ask what the sold-to-asking ratio was on those deals. And references you choose from their deal list, not references they choose for you; the difference between those two lists is where the truth lives.

The ten-deal test

One question separates real specialists from marketing specialists: "Walk me through your last ten closed deals." You're listening for how many were in your industry, the multiples achieved versus asking, and whether they'll connect you with two of those sellers. A genuine specialist answers with specifics and names reference clients happily. A brochure specialist pivots to their marketing process, their proprietary database, or the size of their email list — anything but the deals. Follow up with: "Who are the five most likely buyers for my business, specifically?" Specialists have a list. Generalists have a website. Then verify everything — here's how to check references properly — because the only thing cheaper than claiming a specialty is claiming closed deals.

Frequently asked questions

Do business brokers specialize by industry?

Some do — restaurants, auto, HVAC, healthcare, and IT all have dedicated brokerages. Most Main Street brokers are generalists. Specialists matter most where buyer pools are specific and valuation drivers are technical.

Is an industry-specialist broker worth a higher fee?

Often yes: in our hypothetical, a specialist achieving 3.1× instead of 2.6× on $220K of SDE nets the seller $99,000 more at equal fees, and $85,360 more even charging 12%. But only if the specialty is real — test it first.

How do I know if a broker really knows my industry?

The ten-deal test: last ten closings, how many in your sector, at what multiples, and two reference sellers you can call. Real specialists answer in detail and name likely buyers for your business specifically.
