What Is an Escrow Holdback When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 24, 2026 · 8 min read

An escrow holdback is the slice of your sale price that doesn't come home on closing day. Instead, it sits with a neutral escrow agent — typically for 12 to 24 months — as the buyer's insurance policy: if the inventory count was off, a customer you warranted turns out to have already left, or a tax bill from your watch surfaces, the buyer claims against the escrow instead of chasing you personally. Per SRS Acquiom's deal-terms data, the median indemnity escrow runs 10.0% of the purchase price (8.8% on average). It isn't a penalty and it isn't optional in most deals. It's plumbing — and like plumbing, you only notice it when it's badly installed.

Why buyers insist on it

Every purchase agreement contains representations and warranties: you promise the financials are accurate, the equipment works, the taxes are paid, nobody's suing you. Promises from a seller who has just wired themselves the money and moved to Florida are hard to enforce. An escrow converts your promises into collateral. That's also why holdbacks sized to the deal's actual risk close faster: Axial's dead-deal data shows diligence disputes are the top killer of signed LOIs, and a well-structured escrow is often the tool that lets both sides stop arguing about a risk and just price it.

Escrows travel with a family of related mechanics worth telling apart: the purchase-price adjustment (a closing true-up for working capital — present in more than 90% of deals), the earnout (future payments contingent on performance — about 24% of deals, and only about 1 in 5 earnout dollars ever gets paid), and the seller note (financing you extend). The escrow is the mildest of the bunch: it's your money, already earned, just parked. Our pieces on earnouts and seller financing cover the rowdier siblings.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Teri sells her HVAC company for $800,000. At closing: $800,000 − $80,000 escrow holdback (10%) − $80,000 broker fee (10%) − $6,500 legal and closing costs = a wire of $633,500, about 79% of the headline price.

Month 7: the buyer asserts $14,000 in claims — a compressor Teri warranted failed, and a small sales-tax assessment landed from her ownership period. The escrow agent pays it out per the agreement. Month 12: the remaining $66,000 releases to Teri. Total collected: $633,500 + $66,000 = $699,500, about 87% of the $800,000 everyone toasted at closing. Not a scandal. Just the plumbing working — and a number worth knowing before you spend the headline.

The five terms worth negotiating

Size. 10% is the median, not a law. A clean file — documented add-backs, matching tax returns, a fresh quality of earnings report — is your best argument for 5%. Messy books argue for the buyer.

Length. Indemnity survival typically runs 12–24 months. Shorter is better for you; most legitimate problems surface within a year, conveniently one full tax cycle. Buyers asking for 36 months are pricing fear, not evidence — make them name the specific risk that needs three years to appear.

Staged release. Half out at month 12, the rest at month 18 or 24. Money home early is money that can't be claimed against later.

Baskets and caps. A "basket" (deductible) stops the buyer from nickel-and-diming you over a $900 repair; a cap limits total exposure, often to the escrow itself. Push for the escrow to be the buyer's exclusive remedy for ordinary rep breaches, so a claim ends at the escrow instead of following you home.

Claim mechanics. Written claims only, with documentation, before the deadline, and disputes resolved by a named process. Vague claim language is how $14,000 problems become $40,000 negotiations. This is a job for your own attorney, not just the broker — see whether you need a lawyer to sell. Spoiler: for this clause, yes.

Where the money actually sits, and what it earns

The escrow agent is a neutral third party — often a bank's escrow department, a title company, or a specialist firm — holding the funds under a three-way escrow agreement signed by you, the buyer, and the agent. Neither side can touch the money unilaterally: releases require joint instructions, a final judgment, or the automatic release date passing without claims. The agreement also settles two details sellers forget to ask about. First, interest: escrowed funds can sit in an interest-bearing account, and whoever the agreement names collects that interest — on $80,000 for a year, it's real money, so name yourself. Second, taxes: you may owe tax on escrowed amounts in the year of sale even though the cash arrives later, depending on how the agreement and the installment rules interact. That's a conversation for your CPA before signing, not after — the same conversation that covers how the sale is structured in the first place.

One more distinction worth having straight: the escrow holdback is not the earnest-money deposit. The deposit is the buyer's money, posted early to prove seriousness. The holdback is your money, parked late to guarantee your promises. Confuse the two in negotiation and you'll concede the wrong one — sellers have traded away deposit protections thinking they were shrinking the escrow, and discovered the difference at the worst possible moment.

Keeping the escrow small starts a year earlier

Buyers size holdbacks to fear. Everything that reduces fear shrinks the escrow: tax returns that match the P&L, inventory counted and reconciled, contracts assigned cleanly, no surprise liens. That's diligence-proofing, and it's the same work that defends your price in due diligence — the escrow is just where un-diligenced risk goes to get a price tag. A broker who has closed deals with escrows can tell you what buyers in your size range are currently demanding; it's a fair interview question, alongside the other eight.

Frequently asked questions

How much of the sale price goes into escrow?

Median 10.0%, average 8.8%, held 12–24 months, per SRS Acquiom. On Main Street deals the realistic range is 5–15%, and every term is negotiable.

When do I get my escrow money back?

At the contract's release date, typically 12–24 months after closing, minus documented claims. Staged releases get part of it home sooner.

Can I refuse an escrow holdback?

You can, but expect to pay for the refusal in price or deal risk — more than 90% of deals carry some post-closing adjustment. Shrinking and tightening the escrow beats refusing it.
