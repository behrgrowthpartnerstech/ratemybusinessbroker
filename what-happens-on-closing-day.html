What Happens on Closing Day When You Sell Your Business?

By The Broker Index Team · October 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Closing day is less a ceremony than a choreography: signatures on a stack of documents, a final walk-through and inventory count, payoff letters confirming every lien dies today, the buyer's money landing in escrow, and then the flow of funds — the sequence in which that money pays off debts and fees before the remainder wires to you. Knowing the sequence matters for one blunt reason: the number on the purchase agreement and the number that hits your bank account are not the same number, and sellers who learn that at 4 p.m. on closing day tend to take it personally.

The week before: clearing the runway

By closing week, diligence is done and the lawyers are trading final drafts. Three things have to be true before anyone signs. The lien search has to come back clean or cleanly resolved — your attorney pulls UCC filings, and every lender with a claim on the business's assets issues a payoff letter stating the exact dollar figure that releases it. The lease has to be assigned, with the landlord's consent in writing (landlords are closing day's most reliable source of last-minute drama). And the buyer's financing has to fund — with SBA deals, the bank wires to escrow only after its own checklist clears, which is why closings slip a day or two more often than anyone admits.

The document stack itself: the asset purchase agreement, a bill of sale, assignment of the lease and contracts, your non-compete, a transition services agreement covering your training period, corporate resolutions, and the Form 8594 allocation both sides must file consistently. Most of it is signed electronically now; the "closing table" is increasingly a Tuesday morning of emails and wire confirmations.

The true-up: counting what you're actually handing over

The price was negotiated months ago against a stated inventory and working-capital level. On or near closing day, someone counts. If the deal assumed $60,000 of inventory and the shelves hold $48,000, the price adjusts down $12,000 — and up in the opposite case, which does happen to sellers who kept buying stock out of habit. This isn't sharp practice; purchase price adjustments appear in over 90% of private deals. The walk-through happens the same day: the buyer confirms the equipment on the list is present, working, and not recently swapped for its sadder cousin.

The flow of funds: where the money goes before it reaches you

IBBA data puts cash at close at 76–89% of the price for Main Street deals — the rest rides in seller notes or earnouts. But "cash at close" is the deal's number, not your wire. From it, the escrow agent pays, in order: your business debts (that's what payoff letters are for), the broker's success fee, your share of closing costs, any indemnity escrow the agreement holds back, and the true-up adjustment. What's left is yours.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Dana sells her shop for $450,000: 90% cash at close with a 10% seller note ($45,000), so $405,000 moves today. From escrow: broker fee 10% of the price = $45,000; equipment loan payoff per the payoff letter = $30,000; her half of escrow and closing fees = $5,000; inventory true-up (count came in light) = $12,000.

Wire to Dana: 405,000 − 45,000 − 30,000 − 5,000 − 12,000 = $313,000 — 69.6% of the headline price, with the $45,000 note paying out over coming years, plus interest. Nothing here is a trick; every line was in documents she'd seen. The only question is whether she ran this math in month one or at the closing table.

After the wire: the part nobody photographs

The indemnity escrow — median 10% of the price in 2025 deals — sits with a neutral agent for a year or so, the buyer's insurance against your representations turning out optimistic. Behave as represented and it releases to you on schedule; treat it as money in transit, not money spent. Then comes the transition period your agreement promised: training the buyer, introducing customers and suppliers, handing over passwords nobody wrote down. Do it well — buyers overwhelmingly expect it, your seller note gets repaid by a business that keeps running, and the escrow releases without an argument.

Ask your broker for a pro forma flow of funds in month one, not closing week — every line of Dana's example is knowable the day you list. A broker who produces that spreadsheet unprompted is showing you the professionalism our top-ten list is about; one who waves the question off is auditioning for a different list.

Frequently asked questions

What actually happens on closing day?

Final signatures, walk-through and inventory count, lien payoffs confirmed by payoff letters, buyer's funds landing in escrow, then the flow of funds — debts and fees paid, remainder wired to you. Mostly by email and wire these days.

How much of the price does the seller get at closing?

Deals close at 76–89% cash; the wire is smaller after broker fees, payoffs, holdbacks, and true-ups. On a typical $450K deal, the day-of wire can land near 70% of the headline.

What is an inventory true-up?

A closing-day count that adjusts the price to the actual inventory on hand versus the level the deal assumed. Purchase price adjustments of this kind appear in over 90% of private deals.
