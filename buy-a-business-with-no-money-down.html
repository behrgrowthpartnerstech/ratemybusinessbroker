Can You Really Buy a Business With No Money Down?

By The Broker Index Team · November 18, 2026 · 8 min read

Almost never literally zero — and anyone promising otherwise is usually selling a course, not a business. But the honest answer has two parts. Zero-down deals are rare and tend to smell funny. Low-cash deals are routine and bankable: the SBA requires a 10% equity injection of total project cost, a standby seller note can legally cover up to half of it, and on the median $350,000 deal that puts your cash at closing around $19,500. About 5%. No seminar required.

The SBA floor, and the half you can negotiate away

SBA 7(a) acquisition rules set the equity injection at 10% of total project cost — price plus working capital plus fees, not just the sticker. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, the injection can come partly from limited sources, including seller debt on full standby: no payments at all while the SBA loan is outstanding. Standby seller debt can cover up to half the injection — Starfield Smith's guide to the 8.1 rules walks through the mechanics, and the SBA's own terms page has the program bones. The deal still has to cash-flow: a 1.25 debt-service coverage ratio on historical earnings, not projections. Nothing in the rules requires the 10% to be painful. It just has to be real.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Theo buys a plumbing business at the Q3 medians: price $350,000, SDE $156,000. Total project: $390,000 ($350K + $25K working capital + $15K closing and guaranty fees). Required injection: 10% = $39,000. The seller agrees to a full-standby note for $19,500 — the maximum half — so Theo's cash is the other $19,500. Exactly 5% of the project.

The loan: $351,000 at 10% over 10 years = $4,638 a month, or $55,662 a year. Cash available: $156,000 of SDE minus a $62,000 manager-level salary for Theo = $94,000. Coverage: $94,000 ÷ $55,662 = 1.69× — well past the 1.25 bar. Nobody needed a seminar.

Why would a seller ever agree to full standby?

Because it's often the difference between selling and not selling. A standby note for 5% of the project gets the seller 95% of their money wired at closing — versus waiting for a buyer with the full 10% in cash, in a market where buyers with liquid savings are the scarce resource. The note also signals confidence: a seller who won't stand behind the business for even 5% is telling the buyer's lender something, and lenders listen. The negotiation usually isn't about whether, it's about rate and what happens at the end of the standby period. Get it drafted by the lender's counsel so it actually qualifies — a note with payments, however small, counts as debt, not injection.

Route two: 100% seller financing — rare for a reason

Full seller financing is the only true zero-down structure, and the market prices its opinion of it. Per CT Acquisitions' seller-financing data, notes under $500K typically run 15–30% of the price at 7–9% over 3–5 years — a slice, not the whole pie. Sellers who carry everything face an 11–14% reported default rate, and unsecured notes recover 35–55 cents on the dollar when things go wrong. So when a seller offers to finance 100%, ask the obvious question: a deal only the seller will finance is a deal nobody else would. The honorable exception is the retiring owner carrying paper for a trusted employee — the structure in our employee-and-family sale guide, where the seller knows exactly what they're financing.

Route three: ROBS — your 401(k) buys the business

A ROBS (Rollovers as Business Start-ups) lets your retirement plan buy stock in a new C corporation, which buys the business — no early-withdrawal penalty. The strings: it must be a C corp, you must work in the business as a salaried employee, and the paperwork is real. Per Fit Small Business's provider survey, setup runs $3,000–$5,500 plus roughly $100–150 a month of administration, with minimum rollovers around $50–60K. The IRS runs a dedicated compliance project on ROBS arrangements, which tells you how carefully to do it. And frame it honestly: this isn't no-money-down. It's all-your-money-down — the money just happens to live in a retirement account.

Route four: other people's equity

Investors can fund your injection in exchange for ownership. Keep each under 20% and they generally avoid the personal guarantee, which makes the ask easier. The cost shows up later: a partner owns part of your business forever. Cheap at closing, expensive at exit. One warning the banks enforce: a borrowed injection — a personal loan dressed up as savings — creates two debts feeding on one cash flow, and lenders screen for it the same way sellers screen buyers in our vetting guide.

What the low-cash buyer still needs

Three things, none optional. A credit file around 680 to get read. A deal with 1.25× coverage and room — low cash means no cushion, so buy cushion in the numbers. And a seller willing to negotiate the standby note, which is a conversation your broker can set up — just remember whose side the listing broker is on. If the math never works, the better question might be whether to start instead of buy. Frequently asked questions

Can you buy a business with no money down?

Almost never at zero. The bankable floor is about 5% cash — the SBA's 10% injection with a full-standby seller note covering half. On the median $350K deal, that's roughly $19,500 of your own cash at the closing table.

Can I use my 401(k) without penalty?

Yes, via ROBS: the plan buys C-corp stock, the corp buys the business. Setup $3,000–$5,500 plus monthly fees, salaried-employee requirement, and IRS compliance attention. It's all-your-retirement-down, not no-money-down.

Can the seller cover my down payment?

Up to half of the required injection, if the note is on full standby — no payments while the SBA loan is outstanding. The rest must be yours or another qualifying source, documented well enough that the lender can trace where every dollar came from.
