What Is a Blind Listing? How Brokers Sell Your Business Without Naming It

By The Broker Index Team · November 1, 2026 · 9 min read

A blind listing — also called a blind ad, blind profile, or teaser — is how a business gets advertised for sale without telling the world which business it is. The ad gives a type, a general region, and banded numbers ("established commercial cleaning company, central Ohio, revenue $800–900K"), and nothing that identifies the company: no name, no address, no logo, no photo of the one purple building in town. Buyers who want more sign an NDA and pass a qualification screen; only then does the broker reveal the name. It's the standard mechanism for squaring an impossible-sounding circle: marketing a business to thousands of strangers while keeping the sale a secret.

Why the secrecy is worth this much machinery

Because a leaked sale damages the thing being sold, before any money changes hands. Employees quietly job-hunt, key customers hedge their orders, vendors shorten terms, and competitors tell your accounts you're "shutting down." BizBuySell's confidentiality guidance treats the leak as a pricing event, not a social one — and with businesses spending a median 155 days on the market, the secret has to survive five months of active marketing, not one awkward week. The blind listing is the first layer of that protection; the NDA is the second, and disciplined process — covered in how to sell without employees finding out — is the third.

What goes in a blind profile — and what stays out

A blind profile is short on purpose: a page or two, in Morgan & Westfield's phrase, "usually only several pages" at most. In: industry and business model, a broad region, banded revenue and earnings, years established, headline strengths ("recurring contracts," "manager-run"), and the broker's contact. Out: the name, the address, exact financials, customer names, photos of anything recognizable, and any detail that works like a fingerprint. OffDeal's teaser guide gives the canonical failure: describing a client as one of the "top five craft breweries" in a region — a detail a reader can resolve to a name in one search. Specificity sells, but every notch of specificity spends anonymity, and the exchange rate is brutal.

The reveal staircase

Behind every blind ad is the same three-step staircase. Step one: a buyer responds to the ad. Step two: the NDA plus qualification — a buyer profile and proof of funds, because the name is only shared with people who could plausibly write the check. A sell-side process generates 10–30 signed NDAs, and roughly half of inquirers vanish after first contact — which sounds like failure and is actually the filter doing its job. Step three: the name and the full confidential information memorandum, with the deepest secrets (customer names, exact contracts) held back even then until a letter of intent is signed. Serious-buyer screening is its own craft; here's what qualification should check.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Dario owns a commercial cleaning company: revenue $864,000, SDE $160,000, asking $160,000 × 2.3 = $368,000. His blind profile reads: "Established commercial cleaning company, central Ohio. Revenue $800–900K, discretionary earnings $150K+, asking in the high $300Ks." Bands wide enough that forty companies fit the description.

Why the bands matter: Dario's anchor client is a medical-office group worth 30% of revenue — $259,200 a year. If a leak spooks that client into leaving, the lost account carries roughly $55,000 of SDE with it, and at the same 2.3× multiple the asking price drops by $55,000 × 2.3 = $126,500. The blind listing isn't paranoia. It's a $126,500 insurance policy that costs a page of vagueness.

Where blind ads actually run

Mostly in plain sight. BizBuySell alone carries tens of thousands of listings a year and draws over 3.5 million visits a month, nearly all of them blind profiles exactly like Dario's. The crowd is the camouflage: your employees could scroll past your own business and recognize nothing. Brokers supplement the marketplaces with direct, quiet outreach to likely strategic buyers — under the same NDA discipline. The full map of marketplaces, from BizBuySell to the online-business platforms, is in where to find businesses for sale.

The too-specific trap, and how to test for it

Most blind-listing failures are self-inflicted: the ad that says "family-owned Italian restaurant on the main square, est. 1987" has named itself to everyone in town. Run the dozens-in-metro test before anything publishes — could a motivated reader shortlist fewer than a few dozen candidates from the ad alone? If yes, blur it further: widen the revenue band, loosen the geography, cut the signature detail. The things that make the business identifiable (the awards, the landmark location, the famous client) belong in the CIM behind the NDA, not in the teaser.

And here's the broker-vetting angle, since that's our beat: ask any broker you're interviewing for a sample past teaser, redacted. You're checking two failure modes at once — profiles so specific they out their sellers, and profiles so lazy ("profitable business, USA") they attract nobody. A broker who can't produce one clean, compelling, genuinely blind example hasn't done this often enough. Add it to the interview list.

What a blind listing can't do

Honesty about the limits: a blind profile protects the name, not the deal. It can't screen out a competitor who signs the NDA just to look — that takes the broker's qualification judgment and a stricter competitor-grade NDA. It can't keep a secret through a sloppy process — one CIM forwarded to the wrong inbox undoes five months of discipline. And it can't fix weak fundamentals; vagueness hides your identity, not your margins. Treat the teaser as one gate in a system, with the NDA, the qualification screen, and staged disclosure doing the rest of the work.

Frequently asked questions

What is a blind listing when selling a business?

An ad that markets the business without identifying it: type, region, and banded numbers only. The name is revealed to buyers who sign an NDA and pass qualification.

Why do brokers use blind ads to sell businesses?

Because leaks cost real money — spooked employees, hedging customers, circling competitors — and marketing needs reach anyway. Blind ads deliver the reach while the NDA staircase protects the name.

How do buyers find out which business a blind listing is?

Respond, sign the NDA, pass the qualification screen (profile plus proof of funds), and the broker reveals the name and CIM. About half of inquirers drop out before that point, by design.
