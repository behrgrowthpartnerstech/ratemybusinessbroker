Should You Run a Background Check on Your Business Buyer?

By The Broker Index Team · November 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Yes — and the moment a buyer asks for seller financing, it stops being optional, because you've just become their lender. The vetting kit is five documents: a signed personal financial statement, a credit report, court records, a lien search, and proof of where the down payment comes from. All of it costs less than $200 and an afternoon. Against that: seller-carried notes default at a reported 11–14% rate, and unsecured notes recover just 35–55 cents on the dollar when they do.

The day you become the bank

Per BizBuySell's survey work, 90% of brokers say buyers expect seller financing, while only 29% of sellers plan to offer it — which tells you who usually blinks. On deals under $500K, seller notes typically run 15–30% of the price at 7–9% over 3–5 years. On the $350,000 median sale, that's $52,000–$105,000 of your own money lent to a stranger. Buyers run due diligence on your business for months; this is the same discipline pointed the other way.

The five checks, in order

1. A signed PFS. Assets, liabilities, net worth — with liquid cash broken out. Watch the trust trap: assets moved into trusts within the prior 24 months may be beyond reach if things go wrong later. 2. A credit report. The FCRA requires a permissible purpose; cleanest is the buyer pulling their own and sharing it, or signing a written authorization — the CFPB's guidance covers the rules. 3. Court records. PACER charges 10¢ a page with a $3-per-document cap, waived entirely if you run under $30 a quarter — effectively free. Add your state's court portal. You're looking for a pattern, not one lawsuit; business owners get sued. 4. A UCC lien search at the Secretary of State: who's already in line ahead of you on this buyer's assets. 5. Experience, references, and the source of the down payment. A borrowed injection means two debts feeding on one cash flow. The SBA verifies injection sources on its loans; copy the habit.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Dana sells her machine shop for $400,000: buyer cash plus a $320,000 bank loan, with Dana carrying 20% — an $80,000 note at 8% over 5 years ($1,622/month). The buyer defaults at month 18. Dana has collected $29,198; the balance is $59,251, because amortization front-loads interest. She's the junior creditor behind the bank, stuck behind a 150-day standstill.

If the note is unsecured, the typical 35–55¢ recovery gets her $20,700–$32,600 — a loss of $26,600–$38,500. Secured — personal guarantee plus a UCC-1 filing — recovery runs close to par. The checks that might have flagged this buyer: a shared credit report, a free PACER search, and a $25 lien search.

Check five deserves more than a sentence, because it's the one that predicts whether you'll ever see the back half of your note. Experience: has this buyer run anything — a business, a department, a P&L? A first-time owner isn't disqualifying, but a first-time owner buying a business that runs on skills they don't have is a default with a start date. References: two or three professional ones, actually called. And the down payment's source, in writing: savings is good, a gift letter is fine, a home-equity line is a yellow flag, and a merchant cash advance is a red one — a borrowed injection means two lenders feeding on one cash flow, with you standing last in line. The SBA makes its borrowers document injection sources for exactly this reason. You're extending credit; borrow the habit along with the risk.

The paper that backs the check

Vetting tells you whether to carry the note; the documents decide what a default costs. The standard kit: a personal guarantee, joint and several with the spouse; a UCC-1 filing on the business collateral; a pledge of the buyer entity's stock; covenants — monthly financials within 30 days, 1.25× debt-service coverage, limits on distributions; and an intercreditor standstill negotiated down from 180 days toward 120. None of this is exotic, and all of it is cheaper before closing than after. Your deal attorney drafts it in a day.

Nobody else runs this check for you

The SBA bank vets the buyer for its loan — a 3.2% charge-off rate in FY2024, built on the bank's collateral position, not yours. Your broker gets paid at closing, not when your note performs in year five. The only party with your note's interests at heart is you. That's not cynicism; it's org charts. The lighter-weight version of this screen — proof of funds before the financials go out — is covered in how to tell if a buyer is serious.

Cost of the whole workup, itemized: the PFS is free (the buyer fills it out), the credit report is free-to-$30 depending on route, PACER is effectively free under the quarterly waiver, state court portals run free-to-$10 per search, and the UCC search is roughly $25 at most Secretaries of State. Call it under $200 with lunch included. Against a five-figure note loss at 11–14% odds, it's the best-priced underwriting in American finance — and the buyer who's offended by it has told you something worth knowing at a price you'll never beat.

When to run it

Sequence matters. A light screen — PFS and proof of funds — comes before anyone sees your financials. The full workup — credit, courts, liens, source of funds — comes after you've picked your buyer but before signing LOI exclusivity, while you still have alternatives (and leverage, if you're choosing among multiple offers). And if a buyer refuses the checks? That's not an obstacle to the answer. That is the answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to run a credit check on a buyer?

Yes, with mechanics: the FCRA requires a permissible purpose, and seller financing qualifies. Cleanest routes — the buyer pulls and shares their own report, or signs written authorization first.

What's in a personal financial statement?

Assets, liabilities, and net worth, signed, with liquid cash broken out. Check for assets moved into trusts within the prior 24 months — they may be unreachable in a default.

What if the buyer defaults?

Expect the 11–14% base rate going in. Unsecured notes recover 35–55¢ per dollar; a personal guarantee plus UCC-1 gets you close to par. The documents, not the courtroom, decide the outcome — see our seller-financing guide for the full structure.
