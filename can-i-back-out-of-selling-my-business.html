Can I Back Out of Selling My Business? What It Costs at Every Stage

By The Broker Index Team · October 28, 2026 · 9 min read

Yes — right up until you sign the definitive purchase agreement, you can back out of selling your business. Nobody can force you to sell what you own. The real question is the invoice, and it grows at every stage: backing out before listing costs nothing, backing out of a listing can trigger your broker's full commission if the agreement has a ready-willing-and-able clause, backing out after a signed LOI can owe the buyer $50K–$150K of expense reimbursement, and backing out after the purchase agreement is breach of contract with lawyers attached. Seller cold feet is common enough to have its own statistic: 13.3% of broken LOIs die because the seller walked.

The clause that charges you for not selling

The sneakiest cost sits in the listing agreement you signed on day one. A ready-willing-and-able (RWA) clause says the broker earns their fee by producing a qualified buyer at your price and terms — not by closing. Change your mind after that buyer appears and you can owe the full commission on a sale that never happened. It reads as boilerplate. It isn't.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Gloria lists her bakery at $560,000 with a 10% fee and an RWA clause she didn't negotiate. Four months in, a qualified buyer offers full price, clean terms. Gloria realizes she can't hand over the keys and declines. Under the RWA clause, the broker's fee is already earned: $56,000.

Worse: her signed LOI included a seller-walk expense reimbursement, and the buyer's diligence and legal bills ran $18,500. Total cost of changing her mind: $56,000 + $18,500 = $74,500 — about 13% of the price of a business she still owns. Two sentences of negotiation months earlier (fee payable at closing; reimbursement capped and mutual) would have made the same decision cost almost nothing.

Why sellers walk — and which reasons hold up

Axial's breakdown of 75 broken LOIs reads like a coroner's report: diligence findings 25.3%, quality-of-earnings issues 21.3%, renegotiation attempts 14.7%, seller backed out 13.3%, financing failure 10.7%, business underperformance 8.0% — after an average 106 days under exclusivity.

Source: Axial, Dead Deal Report.

Some walk-aways are wisdom: the buyer started retrading the price mid-diligence, the real post-tax number finally landed, or the business surged and the old ask became wrong. Others are untreated seller psychology — identity, routine, the question of Monday morning. Roughly 75% of exited owners report regret within a year, which argues for doing the emotional diligence before listing, not during someone else's legal diligence. If your hesitation is about the price being right, that's fixable with data; if it's about not wanting to sell, no price fixes it.

Protect the exit before you need it

In the listing agreement: fee payable at closing, not on an RWA standard; a defined termination right with notice (30 days is common); capped marketing reimbursement; and a short, specific tail. Our red-flags guide covers the exact language, and how to fire your broker covers the escape hatch if you skipped this step.

In the LOI: keep walk-away rights explicit, cap any expense reimbursement, keep exclusivity at 60 days with extensions earned by buyer progress, and never let "non-binding" lull you — the binding clauses (no-shop, confidentiality, reimbursement) are the ones that cost money. Details in our LOI guide.

In your head: decide what number and what Monday-morning plan would make you sign, in writing, before the first buyer call. Sellers who can't answer that are the 13.3%. And if you're having doubts mid-process, say so early — buyers forgive a paused deal far more cheaply than a dead one. A good broker will walk you through it rather than pressure you; brokers who pressure you are telling you whose deal this really is. If you're going to walk, walk well

There's a professional way to exit a deal, and it's cheaper than the amateur way. Tell the buyer directly, early, and with a reason — "my family situation changed" costs you nothing; two weeks of unreturned calls costs you a reputation in a small industry where buyers and brokers talk. Offer to cover documented out-of-pocket diligence costs even where the LOI doesn't require it; on Main Street deals that's often $10,000–$25,000, and it converts a potential plaintiff into someone who takes your call in two years when you're ready to sell for real. Put the withdrawal in writing, confirm the NDA still binds both sides, and make sure the broker relationship is squared away under the listing agreement's actual terms rather than a handshake summary of them.

And keep one door open: buyers who were walked away from politely frequently come back. If the issue was price, say so plainly — some of the best deals close six months after the first attempt died, at a number both sides spent the interval getting realistic about.

Frequently asked questions

Can a seller back out after signing a letter of intent?

Usually yes — the deal terms are non-binding. But the binding clauses cost: 60–90 day exclusivity, possible $50K–$150K reimbursement, and a buyer who sank $50K–$200K into diligence and won't return.

Do I owe my broker commission if I decide not to sell?

With an RWA clause, possibly the full fee — $56,000 on a $560,000 listing at 10% — because the fee was "earned" when a qualified buyer appeared. Negotiate fee-on-closing before signing.

When is it too late to back out of selling my business?

After the definitive purchase agreement. From there, walking means breach — damages, possibly specific performance. The real decision windows are before listing, before the LOI, and during diligence.
